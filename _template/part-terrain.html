The title names the space, not the list of things in it

Where we are

A terrain part usually follows the mechanism parts that supply its vocabulary, because the axes are almost always costs that an earlier mechanism established. Name that dependency here.

The readthrough

The failure mode for terrain is the list. Five options enumerated is useless; the same five located on the axes that actually separate them is understanding. So the axes come first — before any item is described — and they are justified, because they are the site's organising claim about the space rather than the book's table of contents.

The axes

  1. The first axis

    Name what varies, and say why this variation is one of the few that matters. An axis chosen badly produces a taxonomy that sorts the items without explaining any of them.

  2. The second axis

    Axes should be as close to independent as the space allows. Two axes that co-vary are one axis and a restatement, and the reader will feel the redundancy without being able to name it.

  3. The third axis

    Three is usually enough. The third axis is very often where the real trade-off lives — the one the book mentions in passing and every practitioner argues about.

Note

Where the field has settled on a different set of axes than the book uses, say so and say which is more useful now. This is the site's organising claim, so it is fair to defend it — but the defence belongs here, not in the readthrough.

The items, placed

Then each item is located on every axis. Not most of them — every one, because a gap in the grid is exactly where a reader asked to place an unlisted item will fail.

The table is the index, not the exposition. Each item still gets continuous prose that says what it actually is and why anyone chose it — the grid tells a reader where an item sits, and the prose tells them what it is like to live there. A terrain part that is only a table has committed the list failure in a different costume.

The first item

Real prose, with the particulars intact: who built it, when, what it was for, what it is called in practice and what it was called in the book. The placement is restated in the prose so a reader who skipped the table is not stranded.

The second item

And so on for every item the book covers. Where the book covers an item so thinly that it cannot be placed on all three axes, say that — a placement inferred by the site rather than supplied by the book is the site's inference and must be labelled as one.

What the axes reveal

Close with what the axes show that the book's own ordering obscures. This is the payoff of having organised the space rather than enumerated it, and it is what makes the placement test passable: someone names an item the book never covers, and the reader can locate it because they have axes rather than a list.

Voice

How the book presents a landscape: whether it orders by history, by convention, by increasing sophistication, or by nothing in particular — and what that ordering does to a reader.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

For terrain: which placements the book supports with evidence, which are received wisdom repeated, and which the site inferred. The third category must be visible.

Notes

Which items have been superseded, which never saw real use despite appearing in every survey, and where the book's presentation reflects the state of the field at publication rather than now.

What you carry forward

The axes, restated compactly — they are what later parts will reuse, and they are what survives after the individual items are forgotten.
