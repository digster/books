Afterword: how it landed, and what the decade did to it

A note on what this page is

Everything up to here has been transmission. This page is assessment, gathered deliberately in one place so that it cannot leak backwards into the reading. Where the parts say what Surfing Uncertainty claims, this says how well it did, what people who understood it objected to, and what has happened since. The book was published in 2016; this is written in 2026, which is long enough for a research programme to show whether it compounds.

How it landed

Well, and quickly. Surfing Uncertainty arrived as the book-length statement the predictive-processing literature had been waiting for, and it was reviewed across philosophy of mind, cognitive science and psychiatry within two years. Reviewers converged on a common assessment: agenda-setting in scope, unusually readable for its difficulty, and more successful at synthesis than at demonstration.

It also arrived with an advantage. Clark's 2013 target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, "Whatever next?", had already put the framework in front of the field with open peer commentary attached, so the objections were in print before the book was. That makes Surfing Uncertainty unusual: it is a book written in knowledge of its own reception, which partly explains why chapter 10 is a list of unsolved problems rather than a victory lap.

The book's real influence has been vocabulary. Terms that were confined to computational neuroscience in 2015 — precision weighting, active inference, prediction error minimisation — are now standard currency in philosophy of mind, psychiatry and parts of clinical practice, and that diffusion runs substantially through this book. Whether the framework is true, it changed what the field argues about.

The strongest critiques

These are given in their best form, from people who understood the book. None of them is the objection that Clark is obviously wrong.

The inductive base is not homogeneous

Matteo Colombo, reviewing in Minds & Machines in 2017, made the sharpest structural objection. Clark's case for predictive processing as a unified theory is built by drawing on three sources at once: Karl Friston's free-energy principle, Bayesian cognitive science, and deep learning. Colombo's point is that these do not exemplify the same kind of mechanism. Deep learning is aimed at pattern extraction from large data sets; Bayesian cognitive science is aimed at building causal models for understanding and action, which is a different task with different success conditions. Worse for the synthesis, biologically-oriented deep learning systems typically learn by backpropagation — in which errors are propagated backwards through the network, which is not what predictive processing says happens. He also notes evidence that brains optimise many distinct cost functions rather than one. If the sources do not exemplify one mechanism, the unification does not follow from them, and his counter-image is a patchwork quilt of models with none enjoying primacy.

The hierarchy is characterised inconsistently

Andrew Buskell, reviewing for the British Society for the Philosophy of Science, pressed a subtler version of the same worry. Clark describes the cortical hierarchy sometimes in terms of spatiotemporal scope, sometimes in terms of context or gist, and sometimes in terms of abstract hyperpriors — and these may not be three aspects of one architecture but three distinct models wearing one name. Buskell also argued, following a framework from Richard Holton, that the top-down/bottom-up contrast Clark relies on functions as a foundational commitment rather than an empirically decidable hypothesis, which limits how far the book can persuade anyone not already sympathetic. His third complaint is the one specialists have repeated most: the treatment of mirror neurons, context, skill and imitation is too brief to satisfy anyone who works on them, a judgement Part 6 reflects.

Precision means too many things

The most damaging critique arrived in 2020, and it lands directly on what this readthrough identified as the framework's load-bearing term. Piotr Litwin and Marcin Miłkowski, writing in Cognitive Science under the title "Unification by Fiat," argued that predictive processing has a theory-implementation gap: the mathematics is strict, but the interpretation of its core terms in psychological or neurobiological vocabulary is, in their phrase, free-for-all. Their central example is precision itself, which across the literature is identified with signal variability, computational confidence, attention, subjective feelings of confidence, and trust — quantities that are not obviously the same thing and whose identification is asserted rather than shown. They add that PP-based models are seldom empirically validated and are frequently offered as just-so stories.

This is worth taking seriously because it is not a complaint about Clark's ambition. It is a claim that the unification is achieved by letting one word cover several distinct phenomena, which is precisely how a framework can appear to explain everything while committing to nothing. The vocabulary note in Part 3 is the small, practical face of this problem.

The embodiment reading is not entailed

Jakob Hohwy's disagreement, set out in Part 7, remains unresolved and is best understood as a demonstration rather than a defeat. Two philosophers of comparable seriousness derive opposite conclusions about the mind's boundaries from identical formalism, which establishes that the formalism does not settle the question. Clark replied at length the year after the book, in a 2017 Noûs paper arguing that the evidentiary boundary is real but mobile — shifting outward to enclose tools and bodily structures as they become transparently reliable. It is an ingenious move. Whether it rescues the extended mind or concedes Hohwy's central point in more agreeable language is exactly what remains in dispute.

And the falsifiability problem, which will not go away

Every critique above is a specific instance of one general worry, and it deserves stating on its own because it is the objection a reader will meet most often. A framework in which the system's own estimate of its confidence determines how much evidence counts can accommodate almost any experimental result by positing the appropriate precision setting after the fact. This readthrough flagged the trade at Part 3 and watched it recur at every level — in the clinical landscape of Part 8, where four mutually adjustable accounts of autism coexist, and in the innate expectations of Part 9, whose content is read off the behaviour they explain.

The strongest form of Clark's defence, and it is a real one: the same charge was levelled at natural selection, at plate tectonics and at the germ theory in their early decades, and the answer in each case was not a knock-down experiment but the accumulation of specific, risky predictions in particular domains. The framework has produced some of those — see below. The honest verdict is that predictive processing is a research programme whose central term is currently doing more work than it has earned, and whose eventual standing depends on whether that term can be pinned down independently of the phenomena it is invoked to explain. Ten years on, it has been pinned down in some places and not in others.

What has held up

Precision-weighted cue combination. The quantitative psychophysics traced in Part 3 was solid before the book and remains so. It is the framework's firmest ground, and notably it is the part that makes numerical predictions rather than qualitative accommodations.

Sensory attenuation, and its failure in psychosis. The prediction that people with schizophrenia should show reduced attenuation of self-produced sensation — and, paradoxically, perform better than controls on certain force-matching tasks — is the kind of risky, counterintuitive claim that distinguishes a working theory from a flexible vocabulary. It has been repeatedly supported, including by imaging work locating the failure.

Conditioned hallucinations. The single best post-publication vindication. In 2017, a year after the book, Albert Powers, Christoph Mathys and Philip Corlett published in Science a study crossing voice-hearing with treatment-seeking in four groups, showing that voice-hearers are markedly more susceptible to Pavlovian-induced hallucinations and that computational modelling localises the difference to the over-weighting of perceptual priors. The framework said where to look, and what was there was what it said would be there.

Functional neurological disorder. The Bayesian account published by Mark Edwards and colleagues in Brain in 2012 has become close to the mainstream framing in neurology, and it changed clinical practice — chiefly by supplying a mechanical account of why the symptoms are genuinely experienced rather than feigned, and why distraction transiently relieves them. Of everything in this book, this is the strand with the clearest record of having done good.

The dual-process dissolution. Clark's argument that model-free and model-based learning are settings rather than systems has been substantially borne out, with subsequent work modelling them as a continuum with arbitration, moving with task demands, stress and load. He backed the right horse, and the broader System 1 / System 2 framing he was writing against has weakened further for independent reasons, including replication failures among the findings that popularised it.

Active inference as a programme. Whatever its truth, the formalism has proved generative — in robotics, in computational psychiatry, and in machine learning, where predictive-coding networks are an active research area precisely because they offer a local learning rule that does not require backpropagation's biologically-awkward global error transport.

What has not, or is still open

The literal cortical implementation. The load-bearing anatomical claim flagged in Part 2 — that cortex contains functionally distinct representation and error populations, wired so each error unit receives its matching prediction — is still not directly demonstrated. Evidence remains largely indirect: model fits to physiology, and effects like endstopping and repetition suppression that are consistent with predictive coding and also with alternatives. Reviews continue to appear asking, in so many words, whether predictive coding is falsifiable as a claim about cortical circuitry. This is the gap that matters most, because every application in the book inherits it.

Autism. The hypo-priors account remains contested. Attempts to demonstrate the predicted attenuation of priors have produced mixed results, with several studies finding intact priors in autistic adults for the very effects the account predicts should be weakened. The rival formulations — over-precise sensory channels, over-fitted predictions, precision imbalance — persist alongside one another, which is exactly the pattern Litwin and Miłkowski describe. The framing objection noted in Part 8 has also grown louder, and reasonably: describing a different perceptual style as a mis-set parameter is a choice, not a finding.

The unification claim itself. Colombo's patchwork-quilt alternative has not been refuted, and the intervening decade has arguably strengthened it. Evidence that the brain optimises many distinct objectives has accumulated rather than diminished, and the deep-learning wing of Clark's inductive base has developed in directions — enormous scale, backpropagation, objectives with no pretence of biological realism — that make it a less comfortable ally than it was in 2016.

The status of the free energy principle. The formalism quarantined into the book's second appendix has had a turbulent decade, with Friston and colleagues substantially reformulating it more than once, and with a persistent critical literature arguing that in its most general form it is a mathematical identity rather than an empirical claim about biology — true of anything that persists, and therefore explanatory of nothing in particular. A reader who took the appendix as optional, as Part 2 warned, will not understand why this argument is so fierce.

Desire. The reduction of motivation to expectation in Part 5 has not been accepted, resolved, or seriously advanced. It remains what it was in 2016: an elegant proposal with an unanswered charge of circularity attached.

What the book is genuinely for

It is the best single statement of predictive processing as a philosophical position — what the framework would mean for mind, agency, experience and the boundaries of the self if it were true. Read it for that, and for the synthesis: nobody else assembled the computational, clinical and embodied literatures into one argument at this length.

What its reputation implies it covers but does not. Three things, worth knowing before you buy it. It is not a technical introduction to predictive coding — the mathematics is in two short appendices and the book will not let you build a model or read the primary computational literature. It is not a neuroscience textbook; the evidence is marshalled to support an argument rather than surveyed, and the reader is not shown the studies that fit badly. And despite the subtitle, it is not principally a book about embodiment: embodiment is the conclusion Clark argues towards in one chapter, not the framework the book is built on. Readers arriving from Being There or Natural-Born Cyborgs expecting more of the same find a considerably more neurocentric book than they anticipated — which is, of course, Hohwy's point.

Where to go next

If you want the argument shorter and with its opposition attached: Clark's 2013 target article "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science" in Behavioral and Brain Sciences 36(3), 181–204. Open peer commentary means you get roughly thirty specialists objecting in print, which is the cheapest available survey of where the framework is weak.

If you want the other philosophical reading: Jakob Hohwy, The Predictive Mind (Oxford, 2013). Same machinery, opposite conclusions about the mind's boundaries, and a more austere and in places more rigorous book. Reading the two together is the best way to see which conclusions come from the formalism and which come from the philosopher.

If you want the accessible version: Clark's own The Experience Machine: How Our Minds Predict and Shape Reality (2023) revisits the same territory for a general audience, with lighter prose and more everyday examples — phantom phone vibrations, chronic pain, social bias. It is a work of transmission rather than discovery, and a reader who has been through these ten parts will find little in it that is new. It is the right recommendation for someone else.

If you want the critique: Litwin and Miłkowski's "Unification by Fiat: Arrested Development of Predictive Processing," Cognitive Science (2020), is open access and is the most useful single corrective to this book. Colombo's review in Minds & Machines (2017) is four pages and does most of the structural work.

If you want the clinical strand: Edwards, Adams, Brown, Pareés and Friston, "A Bayesian account of 'hysteria'," Brain 135(11), 3495–3512, is where this framework has most clearly earned its keep, and it reads well without the philosophy.

And a cross-reference within this shelf: the argument that professional disciplines lose their grip on design when they chase the prestige of natural science is Herbert Simon's, in The Sciences of the Artificial — a book with an unexpectedly close relationship to this one, since Simon's near-decomposability and Clark's hierarchy are two attempts to say why complex systems must be organised in nested levels. When that readthrough lands it will be linked from here.

The one-paragraph verdict

Surfing Uncertainty is a genuinely important book that proves less than it appears to. Its machinery is real, its applications are often illuminating, and in at least four places — cue combination, sensory attenuation, conditioned hallucinations, functional neurological symptoms — it points at findings that a merely flexible vocabulary could not have produced. But its central unification rests on a term whose meaning has not been stabilised, its anatomical commitment remains undemonstrated, and its most attractive philosophical conclusion turns out not to follow from the formalism at all. Clark knew most of this; chapter 10 says so. The book is best read as the strongest available statement of a programme that has not yet been made to fail, and has not yet been made to work.
