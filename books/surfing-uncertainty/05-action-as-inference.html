Action as inference, and the collapse of the sandwich

Where we are

Assumes Part 3 without reservation — this chapter is unreadable without precision. So far the machine has been passive: it receives, predicts, and revises. This part turns it around. Prediction error is a mismatch between expectation and input, and a mismatch can be cancelled from either end; cancelling it by moving the body is what Clark calls active inference, and it is the move that converts a theory of perception into a theory of mind. It also delivers joint 5.

The ratchet

  1. The sandwich, and the arithmetic it cannot afford

    What we have
    The standard architecture, which Clark elsewhere calls the cognitive sandwich: perception at one end, action at the other, and thinking in between. Sensory systems build a representation of the world, central systems decide what to do, motor systems execute. It is intuitive, it maps neatly onto the gross anatomy of sensory and motor cortex, and it organised the field for decades.
    What breaks
    The execution stage, on inspection, is asked to do something close to impossible. To move your hand to a cup, a controller issuing motor commands must solve the inverse dynamics problem: given a desired trajectory, compute the torques each joint must generate. Those torques depend on the limb's current configuration, its inertia, the direction of gravity relative to it, the load in the hand, and the non-linear elastic behaviour of muscle — and every one of those changes continuously as the movement proceeds, including the load, which alters as the cup empties. Worse, the problem is underdetermined in the other direction: the arm has more degrees of freedom than the task constrains, so infinitely many joint trajectories deliver the hand to the same point. A command-issuing controller must therefore solve a hard inverse problem and arbitrarily select among infinitely many solutions, continuously, in real time.
    The fix
    Stop computing torques. Predict instead the proprioceptive consequences the desired movement would have — what the muscle spindles and joint receptors would be reporting if the hand were already at the cup — and let the resulting mismatch be cancelled by machinery that does not need to know any dynamics at all.
    What it costs
    The problem has been relocated rather than abolished. Something must now cancel the mismatch, and something must specify the desired proprioceptive state in the first place.

  2. The cancelling machinery already exists

    What we have
    A descending signal that specifies expected proprioception rather than commanded force.
    What breaks
    A prediction, by itself, moves nothing. Expecting your arm to be somewhere it is not is not a way of getting it there.
    The fix
    Hand the job to the spinal reflex arcs, which have been doing precisely this since long before anyone proposed predictive processing. A muscle spindle signals the muscle's current length; a mismatch between that signal and the level the descending drive has set produces contraction until the mismatch is gone. This is classical motor physiology, and the reading Clark adopts is that the descending signal is best understood as setting the expected sensory state rather than as commanding a force. The movement then happens for the same reason perception happens: something is minimising prediction error. It simply minimises it by changing the world — the position of the arm — rather than by changing the belief.
    What it costs
    An ambiguity that is now structural and urgent. If proprioceptive error can be resolved either by moving or by updating the estimate of where the arm is, what stops the system from taking the cheap option every time?

  3. What decides between believing and moving

    What we have
    Two routes to cancelling the same error, and no principle choosing between them.
    What breaks
    This is not a minor gap; taken naively the scheme is paralysed. Predict that your arm is at the cup, receive proprioceptive evidence that it is at your side, and the cheapest resolution by far is to revise the prediction — to conclude, correctly, that your arm is at your side. The intention evaporates and nothing moves. Every action the framework describes is threatened by the fact that the truth is readily available and the truth is that you have not moved yet.
    The fix
    Precision, doing the most important work it does in this book. During movement, the precision of incoming proprioceptive prediction error is attenuated while the precision of the descending prediction is held high. Sensory evidence about the limb's current position is transiently down-weighted, so it cannot win the argument, and the only remaining route to cancelling the error runs through the reflex arcs. The intention becomes self-fulfilling not because it is powerful but because the evidence against it has been turned down.
    What it costs
    Sensitivity to your own movements, exactly when you are making them. That is a strong, risky, empirical prediction — and it is where this chapter earns its keep, since the prediction was confirmed before it was made.

  4. Where desire went

    What we have
    Movement as the fulfilment of high-precision proprioceptive predictions.
    What breaks
    Nothing in this vocabulary refers to wanting anything. The framework says organisms act to make their predictions true; it does not say they act to obtain food, safety or reward. Yet the entire apparatus of motivation — drives, incentives, the dopaminergic reward machinery — is extremely well evidenced and has to go somewhere.
    The fix
    Clark's answer, and it is the most philosophically loaded move in the chapter: goals are recast as expectations an organism cannot help having about its own states — deeply embedded, high-precision predictions that it will be fed, warm, upright, intact. Acting to satisfy a desire and acting to fulfil a confident prediction become the same thing described twice. Reward is not a separate currency being maximised; it is one of the influences that shapes which expectations get installed and how strongly they are weighted.
    What it costs
    A great deal, and Clark is candid that this is the framework's most exposed flank. It makes desire a species of belief, which many philosophers regard as a category error rather than an economy. And it is very hard to refute: any behaviour whatsoever can be redescribed after the fact as the fulfilment of some expectation. The related worry — why an organism built to minimise surprise does not simply hide — gets its own treatment in Part 9.

A reach, step by step

One complete run with real values. The elbow starts at 20° and the intention installs a proprioceptive prediction of 45°. The reflex loop reduces the mismatch by a fixed proportion of itself on each pass — a loop gain of 0.4, meaning each cycle removes 40% of the error that remains.

  1. Install the prediction. Descending drive sets the expected joint angle to 45°. The spindles report 20°. The comparator sees an error of 45 − 20 = +25°. Note that at this instant the prediction is simply false: the arm is not at 45°. Precision attenuation is what stops that falsity from settling the matter.
  2. First pass. Contraction proportional to the error moves the joint by 0.4 × 25 = 10°, to 30°. The remaining error is +15°.
  3. Second pass. Move by 0.4 × 15 = 6°, to 36°. Error now +9°.
  4. Third pass. Move by 0.4 × 9 = 3.6°, to 39.6°. Error +5.4°.
  5. Fourth pass. Move by 0.4 × 5.4 = 2.16°, to 41.76°. Error +3.24°. The steps are getting smaller in fixed proportion, which is why real reaches decelerate smoothly into the target rather than arriving at speed.
  6. Settling. Continue and the joint reaches 44.58° after eight passes, with a residual error of 0.42° — below any threshold that matters. The movement is complete, and at no point did anything compute a torque, consult the limb's inertia, or select among the infinitely many trajectories that would have worked. The trajectory is whatever falls out of the loop's dynamics.
  7. Release the gate. Once the movement ends, proprioceptive precision returns to normal, and the arm's actual position — now genuinely near 45° — is available as evidence again. The prediction that was false when installed has been made true by the process of acting on it.

What makes this more than a redescription of a servo loop is what it buys: the same quantity, prediction error, and the same weighting parameter, precision, now cover both perception and action. The sandwich has no middle any more, because there is nothing left for the middle to do.

Why you cannot tickle yourself

Turn 3 predicted that the sensory consequences of your own actions should be attenuated. That prediction has an unusually vivid test, and the answer was known before the framework needed it.

In 1998 Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith reported in Nature Neuroscience that a tactile stimulus delivered to the palm is rated as markedly less tickly when the participant produces it themselves than when an identical stimulus is produced externally.1 The apparatus allowed the self-produced movement to be interposed with a delay or a trajectory rotation before it reached the hand, and the rated ticklishness rose steadily as the delay grew — as the delivered sensation diverged further from what the movement predicted. The attenuation is not attached to the fact of self-production. It is attached to predictability, and it degrades in proportion to prediction failure.

The same mechanism has a less charming consequence. In 2003 Sukhi Shergill, Paul Bays, Chris Frith and Daniel Wolpert had pairs of participants take turns pressing on each other's fingers, each instructed simply to match the force they had just received. They did not match it. Because their own force was attenuated in their own perception, each participant had to press harder than the force they received in order to feel that they were matching it — and the exchange escalated rapidly across turns.2 Both parties were reporting their perception honestly, and both were objectively escalating. The paper's title — two eyes for an eye — is the point: a familiar and disagreeable feature of human conflict falls out of a precision setting on a reflex loop.

Note

Sensory attenuation is real and replicated, but the inference from it to active inference specifically is weaker than this chapter's narrative suggests. The result was originally framed within forward-model motor control — a scheme in which an efference copy of the motor command is used to predict and subtract the expected sensory feedback. That account long predates predictive processing, makes the same prediction here, and is not obviously the same theory: it retains genuine motor commands, which active inference explicitly denies. Clark treats forward models as a special case absorbed by the more general framework. That is a defensible reading and it is a reading, not a finding — and readers of the primary literature should know that many motor-control researchers have not adopted it.

Voice

This is where Clark stops sounding like a reporter and starts sounding like an advocate. Chapter 4 has the book's highest density of rhetorical questions and of sentences that begin by conceding a difficulty and end by absorbing it. The register is confident and slightly propulsive, and the metaphors turn kinetic — flow, grip, engagement. It is genuinely exciting writing, and the excitement is doing argumentative work: the reader is carried past the desire problem faster than its difficulty warrants.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that treating descending motor signals as proprioceptive predictions is computationally coherent and dissolves two problems — inverse dynamics and redundant degrees of freedom — that the command model struggles with. And that sensory attenuation during self-produced movement is real, graded by predictability, and has measurable consequences in behaviour.

Asserted: that this is what motor cortex is doing, and that forward-model accounts are a special case rather than a rival. Also asserted, and by some distance the least supported claim in Part I or II of the book, is the reduction of desire to expectation. Clark offers it as an elegant simplification and defends it with plausibility arguments; there is no evidence in the chapter that discriminates it from a view on which value is a genuinely separate quantity. A reader who feels that something has been conjured away here rather than explained is not being uncharitable.

Notes

The force-escalation study is worth pausing on because it is the rare case where this framework makes contact with something outside the laboratory. Its interpretation is genuinely striking: in a tit-for-tat exchange, escalation does not require malice, bad faith, or even misperception of the other party's intent. It requires only that each side perceives its own contribution accurately as it is represented internally, which is to say attenuated. Both parties are honest and both are wrong in the same direction. Whether this generalises beyond finger-pressing to conflicts of consequence is entirely unestablished, and the paper does not claim otherwise; the temptation to over-read it is strong and should be resisted.

What you carry forward

Active inference: prediction error cancelled by moving rather than by believing, with precision attenuation as the switch that decides which. The reach trace, which is the concrete thing to recall when later chapters say "action" and mean this. The collapse of the cognitive sandwich, which is what the rest of the book builds on. And one unresolved debt — the status of desire — which Part 9 has to answer when the dark room arrives. Part 6 takes the same machinery outward, to how acting shapes the sensory stream and how we read the intentions of other people.
