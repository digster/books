The prediction machine

Where we are

Part 1 laid out five joints and named joint 3 — that only the residual travels forward — as the one that turns an old philosophical doctrine into a testable claim about cortex. This part builds the architecture that joints 2 and 3 describe. The aim is that you could draw it from memory and narrate one complete operation through it, because everything in the remaining eight parts is this machine with one parameter changed.

The ratchet

Clark does not present chapter 1 as an escalation — he presents the finished architecture and then motivates it. But an escalation is buried in it, and surfacing it is the difference between knowing that cortex is supposed to work this way and understanding why anyone would propose something so counterintuitive. Four turns, each forced by the failure of the one before.

  1. Feature detection, and the problem it cannot see

    What we have
    A feedforward hierarchy of detectors. Photoreceptors feed cells tuned to oriented edges, which feed cells tuned to contours and conjunctions, which feed cells tuned to shapes and eventually objects. This was not a naive idea; it was the most productive framework in twentieth-century vision science, and it was built on real findings — David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel's orientation-selective cells in cat visual cortex were discovered in the late 1950s and won a Nobel Prize. Each stage extracts more, and the percept is what comes out of the top.
    What breaks
    The inverse problem, made concrete. What reaches the retina at any point is luminance, which is the product of how reflective a surface is and how brightly it is lit. A detector reading luminance cannot separate those two factors, because they arrive multiplied together: a white surface in shadow and a grey surface in sunlight can deliver exactly the same value. Yet what you see, reliably, is the reflectance — the surface colour — not the luminance. No amount of further feedforward processing recovers a quantity that was destroyed at the sensor. Detection cannot do it, because the information is not there to be detected.
    The fix
    Run the problem backwards. Instead of asking what features the signal contains, hold a model of how scenes generate signals and ask which scene would have produced this one. That model can encode the missing constraint — that illumination usually varies smoothly and slowly while reflectance varies sharply — and so recover what the sensor confounded.
    What it costs
    You now need a generative model, which has to be learned and stored, and you need some way of searching for the hypothesis that best fits. Both are expensive, and neither is required by the detection story.

  2. Feedback as modulation, and the redundancy nobody spends

    What we have
    The compromise position, and for decades the mainstream one: a feedforward stream that carries the data, plus descending connections that bias, prime and modulate it. Expectation makes detection faster and lowers thresholds; the data still flows upward.
    What breaks
    Two things, and the second is the one that forces the design. First, the anatomy is out of proportion to the job. Descending cortico-cortical projections are not a small corrective trickle — in many areas they equal or outnumber the ascending ones, and "modulation" is a thin functional description of that much wiring. Second, the forward stream is carrying enormous redundancy. Natural images are highly correlated: neighbouring points are usually similar, and most of what a receptor reports at any instant is the same thing its neighbour just reported. A channel that transmits all of it spends most of its bandwidth on the predictable part. Engineers solved this problem in the 1950s for telephony and television, and the solution has a name: transmit only the difference from what the receiver could already have worked out.
    The fix
    Make the descending signal an actual prediction of the lower level's activity, and make the ascending signal the difference between that prediction and what is really there. Not bias — subtraction. This is predictive coding proper, and it explains the descending anatomy by giving it the main job.
    What it costs
    Two functionally distinct populations are now required at every level — units that carry the current estimate, and units that carry the residual — precisely wired so that each error unit receives its own prediction. It is a much stronger and much more falsifiable anatomical commitment than "modulation."

  3. One level is not enough

    What we have
    A predictor and a residual: one model attempting to explain the sensory signal.
    What breaks
    The causes of a sensory stream are nested, and they run at wildly different speeds. A face is a slow, stable cause; the edge pattern it produces changes every time the head turns or the light shifts. A single-level model must try to predict fast surface detail directly from whatever it knows, which means it must either be astronomically large — one entry per possible configuration — or be wrong most of the time.
    The fix
    Stack the arrangement. Each level predicts the activity of the level below and receives, as its own data, the residual that level could not explain. Slow abstract causes sit near the top, fast concrete ones near the bottom, and each level only has to model the regularities at its own timescale. What ascends is always a complaint about the level below, never raw signal.
    What it costs
    Generality is bought with fragility. Every level is a place the scheme can misfire, and the topmost level has nothing above it to correct it — its expectations, sometimes called hyperpriors, are structurally insulated from revision by data.

  4. Where the model comes from

    What we have
    A hierarchy that infers beautifully, given a model. The model has been assumed.
    What breaks
    Nothing has said where a generative model of the world could come from. Supplying it innately is not credible at this resolution, and supervised learning is not available — nobody labels the visual scene for an infant.
    The fix
    The same error signal does both jobs, at two timescales. On the fast timescale, error revises the current estimate: that is perception. On the slow timescale, error revises the weights that generate predictions: that is learning. The stream supplies its own training signal, because every failed prediction is a labelled example of how the world actually behaves. No teacher is needed.
    What it costs
    The model can only learn from errors it actually makes. A systematic distortion that never produces a residual — because the system's own expectations conceal it — is invisible to the learning rule and can persist indefinitely. This is not a footnote: it is the mechanism behind several of the clinical conditions in Part 8.

One further turn is needed and is not taken in chapter 1: error signals are useless unless the system knows how far to trust them. Clark hands that turn to chapter 2, and so does this readthrough — it is Part 3, and it is the most important page here.

A bar that ends, all the way through

Run one complete operation with real values. The setting is the classic endstopping result: some cells in visual cortex respond well to a short bar of the right orientation and respond less when the bar is extended beyond their receptive field. On the detection story this is awkward. On this machine it is obligatory.

  1. Fix the parameters. The level-2 estimate is r₂ = 0.90, encoding a confident hypothesis that an oriented bar runs continuously through this patch of the visual field. The generative weight linking level 2 to level 1 is U = 1.0, so the descending prediction of level-1 activity is U × r₂ = 0.90. Learning rate for the update is k = 0.5.
  2. Case A, receptive field inside the bar. The sensory drive at level 1 is 0.90. The error unit computes drive minus prediction: 0.90 − 0.90 = 0.00. The residual is zero, so nothing ascends. The stimulus is present, strongly, and the forward signal is silence — because the level above already knew.
  3. Case B, receptive field past the end of the bar. The bar has stopped; the sensory drive falls to 0.10. The descending prediction has not changed — level 2 still believes in a long bar — so it still predicts 0.90. The error unit computes 0.10 − 0.90 = −0.80. That is a large residual, and it ascends.
  4. The update. Level 2 revises its estimate in proportion to the residual it received: r₂ ← 0.90 + 0.5 × 1.0 × (−0.80) = 0.50. The hypothesis has moved from "a long bar" towards "a shorter bar." Present the same stimulus again and the descending prediction is now 0.50, the residual shrinks to 0.10 − 0.50 = −0.40, and the cycle continues until the estimate stops being surprised.
  5. What the recording electrode sees. A unit that is quiet for a long bar and active at the bar's end — which is precisely the endstopping signature, and which the feature-detection story has to treat as a peculiar tuning curve rather than as the system working correctly.

This is not a thought experiment. In 1999 Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard built a hierarchical network on exactly these principles, trained it on natural images, and reported two things in Nature Neuroscience: the representation units developed receptive fields resembling simple cells, and the units carrying the residuals displayed endstopping and other extra-classical receptive-field effects that had been catalogued for decades without a unifying explanation. The effects were not designed in. They fell out of the architecture.

The smallest version that shows the behaviour

Clark's book contains no code. This is the site's reimplementation of the update rule the trace just ran, reduced to the part that matters — a single level, so the recursion is visible without the bookkeeping.

# One level of predictive coding. Perception and learning are the
# same rule applied at two different rates.
def settle(drive, r, U, k=0.5, steps=6):
    for _ in range(steps):
        prediction = U * r
        error      = drive - prediction        # the only thing that ascends
        r          = r + k * U * error          # fast: revise the estimate
        U          = U + 0.01 * error * r     # slow: revise the model
    return r, U

settle(drive=0.10, r=0.90, U=1.0)   # the bar ended; r falls towards 0.10

The highlighted line is the whole architectural claim. Everything else in this book — attention, imagery, action, hallucination — is obtained by changing what happens to that quantity on its way up, rather than by adding new machinery.

Note

Rao and Ballard's result is frequently cited, in Clark and elsewhere, as though it demonstrated that cortex implements predictive coding. It does not, and the distinction matters. It demonstrates that a predictive-coding network trained on natural images reproduces effects that cortex displays. Competing accounts of endstopping in terms of lateral inhibition long predate it and have never been decisively ruled out, and the discriminating experiment — showing that the specific cells carrying these effects are the ones receiving descending predictions — is much harder than the simulation. The evidence is genuinely suggestive and genuinely indirect, and the gap between those is where most of the working disagreement in this field lives.

Where free energy comes in

Clark quarantines Karl Friston's formalism into an appendix, and the decision is defensible — you can operate the machine without it — but the relationship is worth stating plainly, because the two vocabularies are used interchangeably in the literature and the reader will meet both.

What an organism would ideally minimise is surprisal: the improbability of its current sensory state given its model of the world. It cannot compute that directly, because doing so requires averaging over every way the world might have produced the data — an intractable sum. Free energy is a quantity that can be computed from what the system has locally, and that is guaranteed to be an upper bound on surprisal. Push the bound down and you push surprisal down with it, without ever evaluating surprisal. Under the assumption that the relevant distributions are Gaussian, that quantity reduces to something very concrete: a sum of squared prediction errors, each weighted by its precision. Which is to say the machine in this part, and the weighting in the next, are what free-energy minimisation looks like when you write it out. Nothing in the readthrough depends on the derivation; a good deal of the field's rhetoric does.

Voice

Chapter 1 is the most conventionally expository writing in the book: Clark is laying track, and the enthusiasm is held in reserve. He does not ratchet — he states the architecture and then supports it from many directions at once, which is a survey structure rather than an escalating one. He is also careful, here more than later, to distinguish what the framework asserts from what has been shown, a care that thins noticeably as the applications accumulate.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that a predictive-coding hierarchy is computationally coherent, that it can be learned without supervision, and that networks built on it reproduce receptive-field properties real cortex displays. Also that the descending anatomy, which the feedforward story never satisfactorily explained, has an obvious job on this account.

Asserted without justification here: the functional segregation itself. That cortex contains distinct representation and error populations, wired so each error unit receives its matching prediction, is the load-bearing anatomical claim of the entire book — and chapter 1 supports it with consistency arguments and simulation rather than with direct demonstration. Clark's prose is more settled on this point than the evidence is. That is the single most important gap to keep in view while reading the applications, because every one of them inherits it.

Notes

The engineering ancestry is worth knowing, because it makes the idea far less exotic than the neuroscience framing suggests. Predictive coding as a signal-compression technique — transmit only the difference between the signal and what the receiver can already predict — was developed at Bell Labs in the 1950s and is the basis of differential pulse-code modulation, and in various forms of nearly every video codec since. The cortical proposal is the same trick with a different motivation: bandwidth in the one case, inference in the other. Clark notes the affinity; it deserves more weight than he gives it, because it explains why the architecture keeps being independently rediscovered.

On the appendix: relegating the free-energy formalism was a reasonable pedagogical choice but has an unfortunate side effect. A great deal of the criticism aimed at this framework is aimed specifically at the free-energy principle — at whether it is an empirical claim at all, or a mathematical identity that could not be false. A reader who takes the appendix as optional will not see why anyone is angry. The afterword takes this up.

What you carry forward

The machine: descending predictions, ascending residuals, stacked levels, one error signal driving both perception and learning at different rates. The endstopping trace, which is the concrete thing to recall when the architecture feels abstract. And one unpaid debt, named twice now — the residual arriving at a level is meaningless until the system decides how much to trust it. That decision is precision, it is the subject of Part 3, and it is the parameter every later part manipulates.
