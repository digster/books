Sculpting the flow, and reading other people

Where we are

Assumes Part 5. Action has become the fulfilment of proprioceptive predictions, and the sandwich has lost its middle. This part follows the consequence outward in two directions: what it means that an agent can choose its own sensory input, and what happens when the model built to generate your own actions is turned on someone else's.

Sculpting the flow

The chapter's title names a consequence of Part 5 that is easy to state and hard to take seriously enough. If action is available as a way of cancelling prediction error, then an agent is never merely receiving a sensory stream. It is selecting one. Where you put your eyes, how you turn your head, whether you run your fingers across a surface or merely rest them on it — each is a choice about which data will arrive next, and therefore about which of your uncertainties will get resolved.

The clearest case is the saccade. Human vision has high acuity only across a tiny central region, roughly the width of a thumbnail at arm's length, and the eyes relocate that region several times a second — some three or four saccades per second during ordinary viewing, so that a person makes on the order of a hundred and fifty thousand of them in a waking day. The standard framing treats this as a hardware limitation being worked around. The predictive framing treats it as an epistemic action: a movement performed not to change the world but to harvest the specific evidence that would most reduce the model's current uncertainty. On that reading, a saccade is a question, and where people look during a task is a record of what their models did not already know.

The same logic reframes skill. A skilled performer, on this account, is not someone who computes faster. They are someone whose model has learned to keep the sensory stream inside a region it can predict well — positioning themselves early, moving so that the incoming data stays in familiar territory, structuring the task so that surprises do not arise in the first place. Expertise is pre-emption rather than reaction. This is where the book's title metaphor finally earns itself: the surfer does not fight the wave or compute it, but stays continuously in the part of it that keeps them upright, and the whole activity consists of never having to recover.

The joint map: reading other people

The chapter's second and more contentious movement takes the machinery social. The claim is that understanding what another person is doing is not a separate inferential achievement layered on top of seeing them move — it is your own action-generating model, run backwards, on their behaviour. Five conditions, numbered, and the interesting fights are all about number 2.

  1. One model serves both perception and action

    Delivered by Part 5 rather than argued again here. The generative model that predicts the proprioceptive consequences of your movements is the same model that predicts their visual consequences, because it is a model of how an agent like you produces sensory effects of every kind.

  2. The upper levels encode goals, not kinematics

    The load-bearing condition. In a hierarchical model, slow abstract causes sit above fast concrete ones, and for action the slow abstract cause of a movement is what it is for. "Intends to pour" is a cause that persists across a second and a half of continuously changing joint angles; the joint angles are its sensory consequences. If this is right, then inferring the hidden cause of an observed movement is inferring an intention, because intention is what occupies that level of the model.

  3. The model runs in both directions

    Drive it from a goal and it elaborates downwards into the kinematics that would realise it — that is acting. Feed it observed kinematics and let it settle, and it recovers the goal that would have produced them — that is understanding. Nothing is added between the two uses; the difference is which end is clamped.

  4. Precision gating stops inference becoming imitation

    Joints 1 to 3, left alone, predict something plainly false: that watching an action should produce that action, since the model is being driven into the state that generates it. What prevents this is the same switch as in Part 5 — during observation, the precision on proprioceptive prediction error is held low, so the descending predictions are not enacted. Motor resonance without motor output.

  5. Therefore "mirror" activity is the shared model, not a module

    The conclusion. Neurons that fire both when an animal performs an action and when it observes the same action — first reported in macaque premotor cortex in the early 1990s and known ever since as mirror neurons — are not a dedicated social-cognition system that evolution installed. They are what a shared generative model looks like when you record from it in two conditions. The framework predicts their existence rather than accommodating it.

The joints are conjunctive, and joint 2 is carrying the argument. Remove it and every other joint survives intact — the model is still shared, still bidirectional, still precision-gated — but what you recover from watching someone move is a description of the movement, not of the intention behind it. That is motor resonance, and motor resonance is not social understanding. Joint 2 is the entire distance between an echo and a mind-reading, and it is also, not coincidentally, the joint the empirical literature has been arguing about for twenty years.

A hand going to a bottle

Someone across the table reaches for a bottle. Run it through the joints in order.

  1. Joint 1 supplies the model. You have reached for bottles many thousands of times, and in doing so you have learned a generative model relating goals to the whole sensory signature of pursuing them — proprioceptive, but also visual, since you have watched your own hands throughout.
  2. Joint 2 says the hypothesis space is goals. The candidate causes your model entertains at its upper level are not trajectories but purposes: pour a drink, pass it to someone, move it out of the way, throw it. These are the things that stay constant while everything measurable changes.
  3. Joint 3 generates predictions from each candidate. This is the step that makes the account testable, because the candidates predict measurably different kinematics — and they do. Work by Caterina Ansuini, Cristina Becchio, Andrea Cavallo and colleagues has shown that grasping a bottle in order to pour from it produces a hand shape in which the middle and ring fingers are more extended than when the same bottle is grasped in order to displace, throw, or pass it. Intent to move an object rather than to hand it to a person shows up as a smaller maximal finger aperture reached at higher aperture velocity. The intention is not hidden behind the movement. It is in the movement, as a set of small systematic differences.
  4. The residual selects the winner. The observed hand shape — fingers extending — produces small prediction error under "intends to pour" and large error under "intends to pass." The pour hypothesis explains the data away; the others do not. The same competition that resolves binocular rivalry in Part 4 resolves this, and it resolves in favour of a purpose.
  5. Joint 4 keeps your own hand still. Your model has been driven into the state that generates a pouring grasp. The reason you are not now pouring is that proprioceptive precision is attenuated, so the predictions are not enacted. Note the prediction this makes: where that gating is weak or damaged, observation should leak into action. It does — imitation behaviour and echopraxia are recognised consequences of certain frontal lesions, and the ordinary version, in which people unconsciously mimic the postures of those they are talking to, is well documented in healthy adults.
  6. The outcome. You know, before the bottle leaves the table, that a drink is being poured, and you begin moving your glass. The knowledge did not come from a theory about other minds. It came from your own motor model failing to be surprised by one hypothesis and being surprised by the rest — and the reason the timing works, the reason you can move your glass early, is that the model is generative and therefore predicts forward rather than describing what has already happened.

Ansuini and Becchio's group also showed the corresponding fact about observers: intention discrimination tracks these kinematic parameters trial by trial, so that when a particular movement happens to carry the cues weakly, observers judge it worse. That is the pattern a readout account predicts and a pattern that a general-purpose inference about context does not.

Hickok's objection, which grants everything but joint 2

The neuroscientist Gregory Hickok has pressed the strongest sustained case against this family of proposals, in a 2009 paper setting out eight problems for the mirror-neuron account of action understanding and in a 2014 book, The Myth of Mirror Neurons. It is a good contrast case precisely because of what it concedes. Hickok does not deny that motor and perceptual systems share circuitry, nor that observing actions activates motor regions. He denies the inferential step in joint 2: that this activity is what action understanding consists in.

His central evidence is a dissociation. If understanding an observed action requires simulating it in your own motor system, then damaging that system should impair the understanding. Patients with Broca's aphasia have damage in and around the left-hemisphere regions usually identified as the human homologue of the macaque mirror system. Their action comprehension is, on the whole, broadly preserved — the deficit is not remotely as complete as the theory appears to require. The same argument has been run for speech perception in patients with damage to the putative mirror areas.

What does the claim predict with joint 2 removed? That motor activity during observation is real, and is a consequence of understanding rather than its vehicle — the system that recognises what is happening also, incidentally, prepares to do it. On that reading the mirror findings are genuine and their interpretation is inverted, and the lesion data are exactly what you would expect. Giacomo Rizzolatti and Corrado Sinigaglia replied sharply, arguing that Hickok's argument assumes a far simpler and more localised motor system than the anatomy supports, and that partial preservation after partial damage is what a distributed system predicts anyway. The exchange is unusually acid for the literature.

The honest position is that this is unresolved, and that the predictive framing does not resolve it — it inherits it. Clark's version is more defensible than the original mirror-neuron enthusiasm, because it never claimed a dedicated module and can treat motor activity as one contribution among several. But that added modesty comes at the price the contrast exposes: the more readily the account tolerates preserved understanding after motor damage, the less it is claiming that the shared model is doing the work.

Note

Mirror neurons arrived in the 1990s attached to claims of extraordinary breadth — they were going to explain language, empathy, autism, culture and the evolution of mind, and one prominent neuroscientist predicted they would do for psychology what DNA did for biology. Very little of that programme survived contact with evidence. Clark is writing after the deflation and is appropriately careful, but the chapter still leans on the plausibility that mirror findings acquired during the enthusiastic period. It is worth reading the social sections of this chapter with the deflation in mind rather than the original excitement, and worth noting that direct single-cell mirror recordings in humans remain very sparse, for the ordinary reason that the opportunities to make them are rare.

How the cases were selected. The kinematic evidence for joint 2 comes from studies designed to detect intention-specific differences, using constrained laboratory movements — reaching for a bottle on a table with a small set of pre-specified purposes. That design maximises the chance of finding systematic differences and says little about whether the same cues are available in the unconstrained, occluded, badly-lit conditions where real social inference happens. This is not a criticism of the studies, which do what they set out to do; it is a limit on what the chapter can conclude from them.

What the claim buys and pays. It buys a very large economy: no theory-of-mind module, no separate simulation system, no special social machinery — the same model that lets you pour also lets you see pouring. It pays in a specific way that the contrast case exposes. Because the account never claims that motor simulation is necessary for understanding, it is compatible with the lesion data that sank the strong mirror-neuron programme — and equally compatible with the finding that motor involvement contributes nothing. A theory that survives both outcomes has bought its safety by giving up its grip.

Voice

The chapter is where Clark's long-standing interests visibly reassert themselves — action, skill, environmental structure — and the writing loosens accordingly. He moves fast across a lot of ground here and the survey quality is more pronounced than elsewhere: mirror neurons, imitation, skilled performance and social coordination each get a compact treatment rather than a sustained one. Reviewers have found this the least satisfying stretch of the book for that reason, and the judgement seems fair — a specialist in any one of these areas will find their subject handled in a few pages.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that active sensing has a natural and rather elegant home in this framework — eye movements as questions is a genuine reframing that generates research rather than restating findings. And that intentions are, as a matter of measurable fact, expressed in movement kinematics finely enough to be read.

Asserted: that the reading is done by the observer's own motor model. Every piece of evidence in the chapter is consistent with that and none of it discriminates it from the alternative on which a perceptual system learns the kinematic signatures directly, with motor activation as a side effect. Clark's prose treats the simulation reading as the natural interpretation of the shared-model architecture. It is the natural interpretation; it is not an established one, and the chapter does not mark the difference as clearly as chapter 1 marked its equivalents.

Notes

The active-sensing material has aged better than the social material, and it is worth saying why. Treating saccades as uncertainty-reducing actions produced a research programme with quantitative teeth — models that predict where the eyes will go next from the current state of the observer's uncertainty, tested against recorded scanpaths. That is a framework earning its keep. The social material, by contrast, entered a debate that was already deadlocked and did not break it. A reader budgeting attention across this chapter should weight the first half more heavily than its page count suggests.

What you carry forward

Two things. Epistemic action — the idea that a movement can be performed in order to resolve uncertainty rather than to change the world, which returns in Part 9 as part of the answer to the dark room. And the shared-model account of social understanding, with joint 2 marked as the contested one. Part 7 asks what all this modelling actually commits the framework to, which is the question philosophers have been waiting five chapters to ask.
