What kind of representation is this?

Where we are

Five chapters of machinery have been built on a generative model — an internal structure encoding how the world produces sensory data. For readers coming from embodied or enactive cognitive science, that should have been setting off alarms since Part 2, because rejecting rich internal world-models was that tradition's founding move. This part is Clark answering the charge that he has spent twenty years arguing against representations and has now smuggled them back in.

The charge

The tradition Clark belongs to made its name by attacking a particular picture: that intelligent behaviour works by building an accurate internal replica of the environment, reasoning over the replica, and then acting. Rodney Brooks's robots in the 1980s walked without one, and his slogan — that the world is its own best model — became a rallying cry. The argument was partly engineering, that replicas are expensive and stale the moment they are built, and partly conceptual, that the replica picture makes the body a peripheral device rather than a participant.

Predictive processing appears to be the replica picture returning in better mathematics. It posits an internal generative model of the world's causal structure, it treats perception as inference over that model, and it makes the body one more source of sensory data to be explained. Jakob Hohwy, whose 2013 book The Predictive Mind is the other major philosophical treatment of this framework, reads it exactly that way and regards the consequence as welcome. Clark's chapter 6 exists to argue that the reading is wrong, and it is the most purely philosophical chapter in the book.

The joint map

Clark's defence has four conditions and a conclusion. The conclusion is that PP's internal states are action-oriented representations, and that a system built around them is embodied in the sense that matters.

  1. The framework really does posit contentful internal states

    Clark concedes this immediately and without hedging, which is the right move — the alternative would be a semantic evasion. A generative model is a model; the framework's explanations quantify over what a system expects and how confident it is. Any attempt to describe PP as representation-free founders on the fact that precision-weighted inference is not definable without saying what the inference is about.

  2. What they encode is probability over trajectories, not pictures

    The content is not a snapshot. At the upper levels the model holds probability distributions over how sensory states will unfold over time — extended trajectories, with their uncertainties attached, rather than static descriptions of how things currently are. A representation of a cup, on this account, is largely a set of expectations about what would happen next under various interventions. That is a very different object from a replica, and it is already half-way to being a specification for action.

  3. Content is selected for control, not for accuracy

    The model is not trying to be right about the world in general; it is trying to keep an organism's sensory states within survivable bounds. What gets modelled is what makes a difference to that — the organism-salient causal structure of Part 4, not the structure of the world as such. Clark leans here on a result from cybernetics that is old and underused: Roger Conant and Ross Ashby's 1970 theorem that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. The point runs both ways. A controller must model, so representation is not optional — and a controller models only what it must control, so the model is shaped by the regulatory task rather than by the world's own joints.

  4. The loop through body and world is part of the process

    The model is never used the way the replica picture uses one — built, consulted, acted upon. It operates inside a continuous cycle in which acting changes the sensory input, which changes the error, which changes the next prediction, at a timescale where the stages are not separable. On Clark's reading the body and the immediate environment are not merely causes of the sensory data the brain infers from; they are participants in the inference, carrying part of the load that would otherwise fall on the model. This is the joint that does the philosophical work, and it is the one his opponents refuse.

The joints are conjunctive, and joint 4 is carrying the argument — specifically its constitutive reading. Joints 1 to 3 are substantially agreed on by everyone in this debate, including Clark's opponents. Everything turns on whether the body-world loop is part of the cognitive process or merely something the cognitive process is causally hooked up to. Grant the constitutive reading and predictive processing is embodied cognition's best friend. Refuse it, keeping everything else, and the same machinery describes a brain sealed behind its senses, inferring a world it never touches. The machinery does not decide this. That is worth stating plainly, because both sides write as though it does.

Catching a fly ball

The canonical test case for the whole dispute, and one with a real answer. A baseball outfielder runs to where a fly ball will land, and gets there. How?

  1. What the replica picture requires. Estimate the ball's initial velocity and launch angle, apply a model of projectile motion under gravity with air resistance, solve for the landing point, and run there. This is computable but it demands precise initial estimates from a nearly head-on view, which is the worst possible vantage for judging them.
  2. What outfielders actually do. In 1995 Michael McBeath, Dennis Shaffer and Mary Kaiser reported in Science that outfielders run so as to keep the ball moving in a straight line at constant speed in their visual field — they cancel the optical acceleration. They do not run to the landing point. They run a curving path that keeps a perceptual variable constant, and the landing point is simply where they end up when the ball arrives.1 No trajectory is ever computed.
  3. Joint 1: is there a model? Yes. The strategy is not reflex. It requires an expectation about how the optical variable should behave, against which the current value is compared — which is precisely a prediction and a prediction error. An account with no internal expectation cannot say what the runner is nulling.
  4. Joint 2: what does it encode? An expected trajectory of a relational variable over time, with uncertainty. Not the ball's position, not the ball's parabola — the expected unfolding of an optical relationship.
  5. Joint 3: selected for control, not accuracy. This is the decisive observation. The model is silent about the actual physics. It contains no representation of the parabola, no gravitational constant, no launch angle. Judged as a description of the world it is nearly empty. Judged as a controller it is excellent, because it makes the single quantity that determines success — will I be there — directly readable from a variable the runner can act on. A model that is a poor picture and a superb instrument is exactly what joint 3 predicts.
  6. Joint 4: the loop is doing part of the work, constitutively. Here is why this case decides the question rather than illustrating it. The optical acceleration the runner cancels does not exist in the ball and does not exist in the runner. It is a relation between them, and it is brought into being by the running. The movement is not a response to a computed answer; it is the physical medium in which the computation happens, because changing position is what changes the variable's value. Remove the running and there is no error signal to minimise — not a degraded one, none. The body is not supplying data to a process. It is part of the process.
  7. The outcome. A system with a genuine internal model, genuine predictions and genuine error signals, which is nonetheless not remotely the replica picture — and whose competence cannot be described without including the body and the ball. Both halves of Clark's claim, in one case.

Hohwy's secluded brain, which grants 1 to 3

Jakob Hohwy takes the same formalism and reaches the opposite conclusion, and he is not making a mistake — he is refusing joint 4. His argument turns on what he calls the evidentiary boundary. The brain's only access to anything is through its sensory states. Everything on the far side of those states — the ball, the arm, the muscles, the light — is a hidden cause, inferred and never contacted. That boundary is not a metaphor on his account but a real and principled division, because it is exactly where the inference problem is defined.

Run joints 1 to 3 with that boundary in place and everything still holds: contentful states, distributions over trajectories, content shaped by regulatory demands. But joint 4 fails, because the body now sits on the far side of the boundary along with the ball. It is a hidden cause of sensory data, no different in kind from the weather. And action, on this reading, is not enactive coupling with an environment; it is a further inferential process operating on sensory input. What the claim then predicts is a mind that is, in Hohwy's terms, inferentially secluded and neurocentrically skull-bound — the exact opposite of the conclusion Clark draws from the same equations.

Does the record adjudicate? Not cleanly, and this is the honest answer rather than a hedge. The dispute is not about what the brain does; both parties agree about the machinery in every detail that would show up in an experiment. It is about where to draw the boundary of the cognitive system, and that question is prior to the evidence rather than settled by it. What can be said is that the fly-ball case is genuinely awkward for the secluded reading: an account on which the running is merely a cause of sensory input has to explain why the relevant variable is one that exists only while the running is happening. Clark pressed exactly this line in a paper the year after this book, arguing that the evidentiary boundary is real but mobile — that it shifts to enclose tools and bodily structures as they become transparently reliable — which preserves the boundary while denying it marks the edge of the mind.

Note

There is a reasonable suspicion, which several reviewers have voiced, that this chapter's conclusion was fixed before the argument started. Clark has been the leading advocate of embodied and extended cognition for two decades, and it would be remarkable if the framework he adopted turned out to refute his own position — so the finding that predictive processing is "highly extension-friendly" invites scrutiny. Two things can be said in his defence. He engages Hohwy's opposing reading directly and states it fairly rather than in a weakened form. And the action-oriented reading is doing real work elsewhere in the book rather than being bolted on here: it is what makes the lazy brain of Part 9 intelligible. That said, a reader should notice that the chapter establishes compatibility with embodiment and is written as though it established support for it.

How the cases were selected. Sharply, and it matters here more than anywhere else in the book. The cases marshalled for joint 4 are drawn from the sensorimotor repertoire — interception, locomotion, reaching, active touch — where tight body-world coupling is undeniable and where embodied cognition has always been strongest. The cases that made classical representationalism attractive in the first place are absent: planning a route through a city you are not currently in, reasoning about an object's hidden side, remembering an appointment next Tuesday. Those are cognitive achievements that appear to require standing in for what is not present, and a chapter arguing that PP's representations are action-oriented all the way up owes them a treatment it does not supply.

What the claim buys and pays. It buys continuity: Clark keeps his embodied commitments and gains a computational framework, and the field gets a bridge between two traditions that had been talking past each other. It pays in determinacy. Because the disagreement with Hohwy is not empirical, "PP supports embodied cognition" turns out to be a claim no experiment can settle — which means that a reader who came to this chapter hoping the science would resolve a philosophical dispute leaves with a clearer statement of the dispute instead. That is a real result, and it is less than the chapter's tone promises.

Voice

The register shifts here more than anywhere in the book. The enthusiasm drops away and Clark writes like the analytic philosopher he is: distinctions drawn carefully, opponents quoted in their own terms, concessions made early and explicitly. The chapter is noticeably slower and more guarded than chapter 4, and readers who find the surrounding chapters too fluent will find this one the most trustworthy in the book.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that predictive processing is not the replica picture, that its posited contents are structurally different from those embodied cognition attacked, and — via Conant and Ashby — that a controller's need to model is not an embarrassment to embodiment but a consequence of control.

Asserted: that the body-world loop is constitutive of cognition rather than causally coupled to it. This is the load-bearing claim and it is not argued for so much as exhibited, by selecting cases where the constitutive reading is most natural. Hohwy's alternative is presented fairly and then declined rather than defeated. Clark is more entitled to his conclusion than his critics allow and less entitled than the chapter's confident close suggests.

Notes

The Conant and Ashby theorem is worth knowing about independently, because it is routinely misquoted in both directions. It states that the best regulator of a system is necessarily a model of that system, given certain assumptions about optimality and about the regulator's information. It does not state that every regulator contains a rich model, nor that the model must resemble the system in any intuitive sense — a thermostat satisfies it. Used carefully, as Clark uses it, it blocks the claim that a genuinely embodied system can be model-free. Used carelessly, as it often is elsewhere, it becomes a licence to attribute detailed internal world-models to any system that behaves adaptively.

What you carry forward

Action-oriented representation: contentful, probabilistic, trajectory-shaped, and selected by what an organism must control. The fly-ball case, which is the thing to reach for whenever someone asks what a non-replica internal model could possibly be. And the Clark-Hohwy fault line, which is not about the science and which the afterword revisits with what has happened to it since. Part 8 turns the machinery inward — onto the body's own interior, the emotions, and the conditions where the precision machinery breaks.
