The ambitious thesis, and what it would take to be true

Where we are

The beginning. Nothing is assumed except a rough sense of the standard story about perception — that signals arrive at the senses and get progressively assembled into a picture. This part does not yet explain the machinery; it establishes what Clark is claiming, what that claim would need to be true, and which of its conditions is actually carrying the weight. Everything after this is the machinery and its applications.

Meat that predicts

The preface opens with two aliens who cannot believe what they have found. They are from Terry Bisson's 1991 Omni story "They're Made Out of Meat," a piece written entirely as dialogue between two exasperated extraterrestrials trying to file a report on a species that thinks with an organ made of meat. Clark's title for the preface — Meat That Predicts — is doing more than borrowing a joke. The aliens' incredulity is the reader's, aimed at a different target: not that meat should think, but that thinking should turn out to be one specific and rather unglamorous operation performed by that meat, over and over, at every level, for perception and imagination and action alike.

The introduction, Guessing Games, names the operation. Brains guess. They guess continuously, at multiple scales, about what their own sensory surfaces are about to register, and the entire apparatus is organised around detecting and correcting the errors in those guesses. Clark's ambition — and he is explicit that it is an ambition rather than a demonstration — is that this single idea, given the right architecture, delivers a unified account of perception, action, attention, imagination, and a good deal of psychiatry. He calls the package Predictive Processing, and abbreviates it PP.

It is worth being precise about how much of this is new, because the book is often read as claiming more novelty than it does. That perception involves inference is not new; Hermann von Helmholtz argued it in the 1860s, and almost nobody in vision science has seriously disputed it since. What is new — and what makes the thesis ambitious rather than safe — is a set of specific architectural commitments about how the inference is implemented in cortex, and a claim that the same implementation covers domains that have never been treated as perceptual at all. Separating the safe claim from the ambitious one is the whole business of the joint map below, and it is the single most useful thing to have straight before reading the rest of the book.

The joint map

The thesis decomposes into five conditions. They are numbered because the remaining nine parts refer to them by number, and because the interesting disagreements in the literature turn out to be disagreements about particular joints rather than about the thesis as a whole.

  1. The sensory signal underdetermines its causes

    This is the inverse problem, and it is a matter of geometry rather than psychology. A two-dimensional pattern of light on the retina is consistent with indefinitely many three-dimensional arrangements of surfaces, illuminations and viewpoints: a small near object and a large far one project identically, and a surface's brightness confounds how reflective it is with how brightly it is lit. The same holds for a pressure wave at the eardrum, which mixes every sound source in the room into a single scalar over time. So perception cannot be a matter of reading structure off the signal, because the structure is not in the signal. Something has to supply the missing constraint.

  2. A generative model supplies the constraint

    What supplies it is knowledge of how the world produces sensory data — not a catalogue of appearances but a model that runs forwards, from hypothesised causes to their expected sensory consequences. Given a candidate scene, the model says what the retina should be receiving. Perception then becomes a search for the hypothesis whose predicted consequences best match what arrived. Two properties matter for what follows. The model is learnable from the sensory stream itself, without a teacher supplying labels, because the stream provides its own training signal every time a prediction fails. And it is hierarchical: causes at one level are themselves predicted by slower, more abstract causes at the level above.

  3. Only the residual travels forward

    Here is the architectural commitment, and it is where PP stops being a philosophical position and starts being a testable claim about cortex. Descending connections carry predictions. Ascending connections carry only the prediction error — the part of the incoming signal that the descending prediction failed to account for. A perfectly predicted stimulus produces almost no forward signal at all. This inverts the textbook picture, in which forward connections carry the data and backward connections modulate it. On the PP account the forward stream is not the data; it is the complaint.

  4. Error is weighted by expected reliability

    Prediction error cannot be acted on at face value, because sensory channels vary from moment to moment in how much they can be trusted — a visual estimate in fog, a proprioceptive estimate in a numb limb. So each error signal is multiplied by an estimate of the reliability of the channel it came from. That weight is called precision, defined as the inverse of the expected variance. Without joint 4 the scheme is not merely suboptimal but self-destroying, since it would revise its model of the world on the strength of noise. Part 3 is entirely about this joint, and Parts 4, 5, 8 and 9 are all applications of it.

  5. Action minimises the same quantity

    The move that makes the thesis ambitious. Prediction error is a mismatch between what is predicted and what is sensed, and a mismatch can be removed from either end. Change the prediction to fit the sensory data and you have perception; change the sensory data to fit the prediction, by moving, and you have action. Clark takes this seriously enough to accept its strange consequence: motor commands are not commands but proprioceptive predictions, and the movement happens because the reflex arcs cancel the resulting error. Perception and action stop being two systems bridged by a decision and become two directions of one operation.

The joints are conjunctive, and joint 3 is carrying the argument — though joint 4 is carrying the reach. That distinction is worth holding onto. Remove joint 3 and you are left with a claim substantially identical to Helmholtz's: that perception is inference constrained by prior knowledge. That claim is old, widely accepted, and makes almost no commitments about brains. Joint 3 is what turns it into a research programme, because it predicts specific and checkable things about which cells fire when. Joint 4, meanwhile, is what lets one mechanism stretch across attention, imagery, action, autism and psychosis — every one of those applications is a story about a precision setting. Joint 3 makes the thesis testable; joint 4 makes it ambitious.

Five days in a blindfold, joint by joint

Take the case Clark uses to unsettle the standard picture before he has argued against it: what happens when you take the sensory data away. Intuition says perception should stop, or thin out towards nothing. Run it through the joints instead.

  1. Joint 1 is present, in the limit. In 2004 Lotfi Merabet and colleagues fitted 13 healthy sighted volunteers — no psychiatric history, no ocular pathology — with a sealed blindfold and kept it on for five consecutive days, ninety-six hours, with subjects dictating their experiences into a handheld recorder.1 Underdetermination is normally partial: the signal is consistent with many world-states. Here it is total. The signal is consistent with every world-state, because there is no signal.
  2. Joint 2 is untouched. The generative model does not depend on input for its existence. It is a standing structure — learned over a lifetime, encoded in the strengths of descending connections — and it continues to generate predictions about what the visual system should be registering whether or not anything is registering. Nothing about the blindfold reaches down and switches it off.
  3. Joint 3 is present, and now decisive. The only thing that ever corrects a descending prediction is the residual that ascends when the prediction is wrong. Blindfolded, there is no residual. So there is nothing in the system capable of saying no, not that — the ordinary corrective traffic has gone silent, and the descending model runs uncontested.
  4. Joint 4 explains the character of what happens next. Precision on the visual channel collapses, because the system's own estimate of that channel's reliability falls to near zero. With the sensory weight at the floor, the prior does all the work, and the content of experience becomes whatever the generative model most expects to see. Since that model was trained on a world containing faces, landscapes and objects, that is what it produces.
  5. The outcome follows from the structure. Ten of the thirteen subjects — 77% — reported visual hallucinations. Not noise or blankness: both simple forms, bright spots and flashes of light, and fully complex ones — faces, landscapes, ornate objects. The standard bottom-up picture predicts an absence. The joints predict a presence, and specifically a structured presence drawn from the statistics of the world the model was trained on. That is what the record shows.

The same pattern shows up on a shorter timescale. Oliver Mason and Francesca Brady put 19 participants in an anechoic chamber — total deprivation of both light and sound — and elicited perceptual disturbances, paranoia and other psychotic-like experiences after as little as 15 minutes, most strongly in those already prone to hallucinatory experience.

Helmholtz, who had three of the five

Now the case that shows which joint is doing the work. In the 1860s, in the Handbuch der physiologischen Optik, Hermann von Helmholtz argued that perception is unbewusster Schluss — unconscious inference.2 He had joint 1 outright: he understood the inverse problem better than anyone of his generation, having done the optics himself. He had a workable version of joint 2, since the inference he described was constrained by knowledge of how objects ordinarily produce retinal images. He arguably had a version of joint 5, given his interest in how eye movements contribute to spatial perception.

He did not have joint 3, and could not have — the anatomy was not known. And that is the difference between a doctrine and a research programme. What does the claim predict once joint 3 is removed? That perception is inferential, that priors matter, that illusions reveal assumptions. All true, all confirmed, and none of it constraining: it is compatible with essentially any implementation, which is why the doctrine could sit largely unchanged for a century while vision science was rebuilt around it twice. It generates no expectation about what a cell in V1 should do when its stimulus is perfectly predicted, because it says nothing about what ascending fibres carry.

Add joint 3 and the claim acquires teeth. It now predicts that perfectly predictable stimulation should reduce forward firing, that the surround effects long catalogued in visual cortex should be signatures of failed prediction rather than of feedforward feature detection, and that the massive descending projections anatomists had been unable to assign a function to are the carriers of the model. Those are checkable, and Part 2 checks them. The record broadly agrees, with qualifications the afterword takes up.

Note

The Mason and Brady study is a useful particular but a thin one, and Clark's framing does not flag how thin. It is a pilot with 19 participants total, split across conditions, using self-report on scales designed for a different purpose. It is entirely consistent with the joints; it establishes very little on its own. The Merabet blindfold study is the stronger of the two — longer, better controlled, with a striking base rate — but note what neither can do: both are consistent with any account on which reduced input releases spontaneous activity, including accounts with no generative model in them at all. Deprivation hallucinations sit comfortably inside PP without being evidence that discriminates it from its rivals.

The support ledger

What is actually offered for each joint, of what type, and how much it can bear. This table is a map of where the argument is strong and where it is being asked to hold on credit; the parts that follow fill in the cells.

4 Quantitative cue-combination psychophysics; attentional gain effects psychophysics The strongest support in the book — precise, numerical, replicated
5 Motor neuroscience of reflex arcs and sensory attenuation; the free-energy derivation theory plus indirect physiology Elegant and coherent; its most compelling form is formal rather than empirical

How the cases were selected. Clark's method is avowedly an inference to the best explanation, assembled by surveying phenomena across many domains and showing that PP accommodates them. The selection is therefore not neutral: the book gathers cases PP handles well, and does not systematically hunt for cases it handles badly. This is not a hidden flaw — Clark is open about the strategy, and chapter 10 lists outstanding problems — but it is a specification of what the evidence can establish. A survey of successes can demonstrate that a framework is powerful and wide-ranging. It cannot, by itself, establish that the framework is better than an unexamined alternative, because the alternative was not put through the same survey.

What the claim buys and pays. The reach is purchased with elasticity, and the currency is joint 4. Precision is a free parameter that the system estimates for itself, which means that for almost any experimental result there exists a precision setting that accommodates it. That is a genuine explanatory resource — it is what lets one mechanism cover attention and autism and dreaming — and it is exactly what critics point to when they argue the framework cannot be refuted by anything. Both readings are available from the same feature, and the honest position is to notice that the property which makes chapters 4 through 9 possible is the property that makes chapter 10 necessary.

Voice

Clark writes with a genial, ranging enthusiasm and a habit of reaching for vivid physical metaphors — surfing, dancing, sculpting — at exactly the moments where the underlying claim is at its most abstract. He is generous with other people's work to a fault, frequently framing a contested proposal in its most attractive form before noting the dispute in a subordinate clause. The recurring rhetorical move is the reveal: set up a familiar phenomenon, show it dissolving into the prediction-error story, and let the reader feel the framework absorb another domain. That move is genuinely illuminating and it is also, cumulatively, the book's principal instrument of persuasion.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that the ambitious thesis has five separable conditions, that joints 1 and 2 are near-consensus positions inherited from a long tradition, and that the interesting content lives in joints 3, 4 and 5.

Asserted, and to be discharged later: everything about implementation. This part has not shown that cortex works this way, only what would have to be true for it to. Clark's own preface and introduction are similarly forward-leaning; they promise a unified science and describe its shape without yet supplying the machinery. A reader who finds the introduction over-confident is reading it correctly — it is a prospectus, and the prose is more certain than anything argued at that point warrants.

Notes

The word "unified" does a great deal of work in this book, and it is worth deciding early which of two things it means. It might mean that one mechanism — literally the same cortical computation — implements perception, action, attention and imagery. Or it might mean that one formal description covers all of them, in the way that a variational principle covers optics and mechanics without those being the same physical process. Clark generally intends the stronger reading. Matteo Colombo, reviewing the book in Minds & Machines in 2017, argued that the evidence assembled will not support it: the sources Clark draws on are heterogeneous, deep-learning systems and Bayesian model-builders are aimed at different tasks, and current neuroscience suggests brains optimise many distinct cost functions rather than one. His alternative image is a patchwork quilt of models with no single one enjoying primacy. That objection is taken up properly in the afterword; it is flagged here because the strong reading of "unified" is installed in the introduction, long before it is defended.

What you carry forward

Five numbered joints, referred to by number from here on. The distinction between the safe claim (perception is inference) and the ambitious one (a specific cortical architecture implements it, and the same architecture covers action). And one expectation to test as you read: that essentially every application in Parts II and III of the book will turn out to be a story about joint 4. Part 2 builds the architecture that joints 2 and 3 describe, and shows why anyone would have arrived at such a strange design.
