Expecting ourselves: the clinical landscape

Where we are

Assumes Part 3 and Part 5. The machinery has been pointed outward at the world and at other people. Chapter 7 turns it inward — onto the body's own interior, onto emotion, onto the sense of being the author of your actions — and then onto what happens when the precision machinery is mis-set. This is the book's most clinically consequential chapter and the one where its explanatory reach is most visible, for better and worse.

Turning the machine inward

Everything so far has treated sensory signals as reports about the world outside. But the same cortical arrangement receives a second and much older class of signal: interoception, the stream reporting the state of the body's interior — heart rate, gut distension, respiration, blood chemistry, inflammation, temperature. The extension Clark adopts is that these signals are handled exactly like any others. There are descending predictions about what the viscera should be doing, ascending errors when they are not, and precision weights governing how much those errors count.

The consequence is a theory of emotion. An interoceptive signal is as ambiguous as a retinal image: a raised heart rate is consistent with fear, exertion, arousal, caffeine, or illness. Under this framework an emotion is the brain's best explanation of its own interoceptive data given the current context — inference about bodily state, not perception of a pre-existing feeling. This is close to what Anil Seth has developed as interoceptive predictive coding and to Lisa Feldman Barrett's constructionist account of emotion, and it inherits a much older lineage running back to William James's proposal that we do not tremble because we are afraid but are afraid because we tremble. The predictive version adds what James lacked: an account of why the same bodily state supports different emotions on different occasions, since the prior does the disambiguating.

The same move gives an account of the sense of agency. Recall from Part 5 that self-produced sensations are attenuated. That attenuation is not merely an efficiency — it is, on this account, the mark by which actions are recognised as one's own. A movement whose sensory consequences were predicted and damped feels like something you did. A movement whose consequences arrive at full strength feels like something that happened to you. Ownership of action becomes a precision signature rather than an extra judgement laid on top.

Chapter 7 also gestures at consciousness — at whether the framework has anything to say about the hard problem. Clark is notably more restrained here than elsewhere, and the gesture is a genuine gesture rather than a proposal: the suggestion is that predictive processing constrains what a theory of conscious content would have to look like, not that it explains why there is experience at all. It is the most cautious page in the book and the caution is warranted.

The axes

What follows is a landscape of conditions, and the temptation is to list them — autism here, schizophrenia there, placebo somewhere else. That would be useless, because the clinical categories are not what the framework organises. Three axes organise it, and they are chosen because they are the parameters the machinery actually has. Everything this framework can say about a condition is a statement about where it sits on these three.

  1. Which side of the balance is disturbed

    Not the precision of priors alone, and not the precision of sensory prediction error alone, but the ratio between them. This is the right quantity because it is the only one that determines an outcome: a prior weighted at ten against evidence weighted at one behaves identically to a prior at one against evidence at a tenth. Clinically indistinguishable mechanisms — over-trusting expectation, under-trusting the senses — are the same point on this axis, which is exactly why the literature contains apparently rival accounts of the same condition that turn out to be notational variants. Call the two directions prior-dominant and evidence-dominant.

  2. Where in the hierarchy the imbalance sits

    The same imbalance produces radically different symptoms at different levels, so a one-dimensional account cannot work. A prior-dominant setting low in the hierarchy distorts what is sensed — a sound heard that was not made. The same setting high in the hierarchy distorts what is believed — an explanation held against all evidence. These are different clinical phenomena and they can dissociate, which is why hallucination and delusion, long bundled together as "positive symptoms," are two placements rather than one.

  3. Which stream carries it

    Exteroceptive, proprioceptive, or interoceptive. This axis is the one the book's inward turn buys, and it is what converts a theory of perception into a theory of psychiatry. The same imbalance at the same level presents as a perceptual symptom in the exteroceptive stream, as a disorder of agency or movement in the proprioceptive stream, and as an affective or bodily symptom in the interoceptive stream. Without this axis, conditions as different as auditory hallucination and panic look like the same fault; with it, they are the same fault in different channels.

Note

These axes are the site's organising claim about the space, not the book's. Clark's chapter proceeds condition by condition, in the order the literature developed, which obscures the fact that several of the conditions are neighbours. The axes are constructed from parameters the framework genuinely has, and every placement below is derived from published accounts — but the grid is an editorial imposition and a reader should hold it as one. Where a placement is the site's inference rather than a claim in the literature, it is marked.

Ordinary attention

The origin of the space, and it belongs in the table because without it the clinical entries look like a catalogue of faults rather than positions on a continuum that includes health. Attending is moving along axis 1 transiently and reversibly, wherever axis 2 and axis 3 require. The pathological placements below differ from it in being stuck — a setting that will not return — rather than in being a different kind of thing. That is the framework's central clinical claim and its most humane consequence.

Hallucination in psychosis

Prior-dominant, low in the hierarchy, usually in the auditory stream. Expectation overwhelms evidence at a perceptual level, and the winning hypothesis is experienced rather than merely entertained — the same resolution as the White Christmas result in Part 3, but chronic and unbidden rather than induced by an experimenter's sentence. The framework's strongest moment here came after the book was published. In 2017 Albert Powers, Christoph Mathys and Philip Corlett reported in Science that people who hear voices are substantially more susceptible to conditioned hallucinations — trained to associate a light with a tone, they went on to report hearing the tone when only the light appeared — and that computational modelling located the difference precisely where the framework says it should be, in the weighting of perceptual priors. The design was unusually careful: four groups crossing voice-hearing against treatment-seeking, which separates the perceptual anomaly from the fact of being ill.

Delusion

The same imbalance, high in the hierarchy. What is over-weighted is not a perceptual expectation but an explanatory one, and the result is a belief that survives disconfirming evidence — because on this account the evidence is not being ignored, it is being assigned a precision too low to move anything. This is where the cost identified in Part 2 arrives: a model that sets how far to trust the evidence against it can, at the limit, make itself unreachable. Note the useful consequence that delusion and hallucination are separable placements, which matches the clinical fact that they dissociate.

Delusions of control

The most elegant entry in the landscape, because it sits on the opposite side of axis 1 from the other psychotic symptoms and thereby explains something the older literature found awkward. Passivity experiences — the conviction that one's movements are being made by an external agent — follow directly from a failure of the sensory attenuation described in Part 5. If the consequences of your own movements arrive unattenuated, they carry the signature of externally caused events, and the experience of alien control is the correct inference from a corrupted signal rather than a bizarre belief.

This makes a counterintuitive prediction: people with schizophrenia should be less susceptible to the self-produced attenuation effects that everyone else shows, and therefore in certain narrow laboratory tasks should be more accurate than healthy controls. Studies from the group that produced the force-escalation work have reported impaired sensory prediction in schizophrenia, including a 2014 imaging study by Sukhi Shergill and colleagues in JAMA Psychiatry tracking where in the brain the failure sits.1 A theory that predicts a patient group will outperform controls, and is then borne out, has done something that accommodating frameworks cannot.

Autism

The most contested placement in the landscape, and the one where the framework's flexibility is most exposed. The influential proposal came from Elizabeth Pellicano and David Burr in 2012 in Trends in Cognitive Sciences, arguing that autistic perception involves attenuated priors — hypo-priors — so that experience is less shaped by prior expectation and more directly determined by incoming data. Their phrase for the consequence has stuck: the world becomes too real. It accounts naturally for superior performance on tasks where context normally misleads, and for the aversiveness of sensory environments that others find unremarkable.

It was contested immediately, and the alternatives are instructive because they are not really rivals. Jon Brock proposed that the priors are intact and the sensory channels are over-precise. Sander Van de Cruys and colleagues developed an account on which the problem is not weak priors but over-fitted ones — predictions that are strong yet fail to generalise. Rebecca Lawson, Geraint Rees and Karl Friston framed it as an imbalance of precision between the two. Locate these on axis 1 and three of the four are the same placement described from different ends, which is the point the axis was chosen to expose. The genuine disagreement is narrower than the volume of argument suggests, and it concerns whether the fault is in the precision estimates or in the priors themselves.

Placebo analgesia

A prior-dominant placement in the interoceptive stream, and the only one in this landscape that is induced deliberately and for benefit. An expectation of relief, installed by ritual, authority and context, is given high precision; the nociceptive error signal is weighted down; and the resulting percept is of less pain. The framework's contribution here is to insist the effect is perceptual rather than reportorial — the patient is not being polite, they are experiencing less pain, because the percept is the precision-weighted resolution and the prior won. The same machinery run with a negative expectation gives nocebo, where warning someone about a side effect helps produce it.

Functional neurological symptoms

Weakness, tremor, or sensory loss without structural disease — historically called hysterical or psychogenic, and historically treated with suspicion about whether the patient was feigning. The Bayesian account, set out by Mark Edwards, Rick Adams, Harriet Brown, Isabel Pareés and Karl Friston in Brain in 2012,2 places these at a mid level of the hierarchy in the proprioceptive stream: a high-precision prior about the body's state — shaped by symptom expectation, illness beliefs, and body-focused attention — which is strong enough to determine perception and movement.

Two things make this the most consequential entry in the chapter. The symptoms are genuinely experienced, not feigned, and the account says why in mechanical terms rather than as a courtesy. And the role of body-focused attention is precision-raising, which makes an unusual clinical prediction: directing attention away from the affected limb should transiently reduce the symptom, which is exactly the distractibility neurologists use diagnostically.

Anxiety

Prior-dominant for threat, high in the hierarchy but driving low, in the interoceptive stream. A strongly weighted expectation of danger generates predictions of the bodily state that danger would produce; active inference then makes those predictions true, since the autonomic system can be recruited to fulfil an interoceptive prediction just as the muscles fulfil a proprioceptive one. The racing heart is not evidence of a threat that was detected. It is the fulfilment of a prediction, and it then serves as evidence for the expectation that produced it. This placement is partly the site's inference: Clark's treatment of anxiety is briefer than his treatment of psychosis, and the self-confirming loop is more explicit in the later clinical literature than in the chapter.

What the axes reveal

Three things the book's own condition-by-condition ordering conceals.

Psychosis is not one placement. Hallucination and delusion share axis 1 and differ on axis 2; delusions of control sit on the opposite side of axis 1 from both. A single disorder therefore spans the space rather than occupying a point, which is a real result: it predicts that these symptom clusters should dissociate, respond differently, and have partly separate mechanisms — and they do.

Several apparently rival theories are the same theory. The autism debate is the clearest case. Once axis 1 is defined as a ratio, "weak priors" and "over-precise sensory channels" stop being alternatives. That is worth knowing before reading twenty years of commentary as though it were a controversy.

The landscape has no natural boundary around illness. Ordinary attention, placebo, hypnotic suggestion, expertise, anxiety and psychosis all live on the same three axes and differ in degree and persistence. This is the framework's most attractive feature clinically and its most dangerous feature intellectually, and both should be held at once.

Now the test the axes exist to pass — three conditions the chapter never mentions. Tinnitus: prior-dominant, low, exteroceptive auditory — a perceptual prior unopposed after hearing loss removes the evidence, which is why it follows deafness and why it resembles the Charles Bonnet case in Part 4 transposed to another sense. Phantom limb pain: prior-dominant, mid, proprioceptive and nociceptive — a standing model of a limb that no longer sends error signals capable of correcting it, which is why treatments that supply substitute visual evidence, such as mirror therapy, do anything at all. Chronic fatigue and related conditions: prior-dominant, mid-to-high, interoceptive — a strongly weighted model of bodily state that predicts exhaustion, with active inference making the prediction true. Note that the third placement is contested and politically fraught in a way the first two are not, and that the framework's tidiness here should increase caution rather than confidence.

Voice

Clark is more careful in this chapter than the material's drama invites, and it shows in the hedging: proposals are attributed to the clinicians and modellers who made them, and the framework is offered as an organising scheme rather than as a diagnosis. He is visibly aware that a philosopher writing about psychiatric conditions is on borrowed ground. The consciousness material at the chapter's end is the most tentative writing in the book.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that a single parameter provides a common vocabulary for conditions previously described in unrelated languages, and that this vocabulary generated at least one risky, counterintuitive and confirmed prediction — reduced sensory attenuation in schizophrenia, with its paradoxical laboratory consequence. That is the strongest evidence in the book that the framework is doing work rather than relabelling.

Asserted: that these placements are mechanisms rather than redescriptions. For most of the landscape the framework has not predicted anything that was not already known, and the placements were constructed to fit conditions whose features were established first. The autism entry is the honest case: four accounts, mutually adjustable, none currently distinguishable by evidence. Clark presents the landscape's coherence as support for the framework. Coherence across many conditions is exactly what a sufficiently flexible parameter would also produce, and chapter 7 does not separate these.

Notes

The autism material needs a caveat the chapter does not supply. The hypo-priors proposal was influential and remains unsettled: attempts to demonstrate the predicted weak priors have produced mixed results, with several studies finding priors intact in autistic adults for the very perceptual effects the account predicts should be attenuated. There is also a framing concern worth stating separately from the evidence. Accounts of this kind describe autistic perception as a parameter set incorrectly, and many autistic people and researchers object to the deficit framing, arguing that a different precision setting is a different way of perceiving with characteristic advantages — better performance where context misleads — rather than a malfunction. The framework itself is neutral on this; the vocabulary of "faults" is not, and this readthrough's axes inherit the problem.

On placebo: the perceptual reading is well supported for pain and much weaker elsewhere. Placebo effects on objectively measured outcomes — tumour size, infection, lung function — are small or absent, and the framework predicts exactly that, since precision weighting changes what is inferred from bodily signals rather than what the body is doing. It is worth noticing that this is a case where the framework's limits are a point in its favour.

What you carry forward

Three axes — balance, level, stream — and the ability to place a condition the book never named. Interoceptive inference, which is what lets this framework reach emotion and psychiatry at all. And the recognition that attention, placebo and psychosis are neighbours on one continuum. Part 9 opens Part III of the book, dissolves the two-systems picture of reasoning, and finally confronts the objection that has been waiting since Part 5: why a surprise-minimising creature does not simply hide.
