The move: culture as error-minimising engineering

The reframing chapter 9 offers is simple to state and does a surprising amount of work. Human beings are surrounded by structures we made and did not individually design — words, conventions, laws, timetables, queues, currencies, buildings. The standard way to think about these is as transmitted content: things a culture knows, passed between generations. Clark's proposal is that they are better understood as prediction-error-minimising devices. We build and continually rebuild them so that other people become predictable to us and we become predictable to them.

Seen this way a norm is not primarily a rule with moral force. It is a device that collapses the space of what someone might do next into something a model can anticipate cheaply — and the discomfort of a violated convention is, on this account, prediction error with an affective signature. A queue is an engineering solution to the problem of predicting a crowd. Standardised time is one of the largest such devices ever built, and the history shows the motive plainly. Through the nineteenth century each American town kept its own solar noon, and each railroad ran on a clock of its own choosing — an arrangement that produced collisions. On 11 October 1883 the General Time Convention met at the Grand Pacific Hotel in Chicago, where William F. Allen proposed dividing the continent into zones an hour apart, based on the meridians 75, 90, 105 and 120 degrees west of Greenwich. The railroads voted for it, and at noon on 18 November 1883 station clocks were reset across the country — in many cities the hour struck twice, once on local time and once on the new standard, which is why the day is remembered as the Day of Two Noons. Nobody discovered anything about time. A very large source of mutual unpredictability was legislated away.

The axes

The chapter moves quickly across a lot of cultural material, and listing what it mentions would transmit nothing. Three axes organise the space, chosen because each names a genuinely different way a structure outside the head can reduce error inside it — and because together they predict what a scaffold will and will not do for you.

  1. Which uncertainty it reduces

    About the world, about other people, or about one's own future states. This matters because the three have different failure modes: a device that makes the physical world predictable keeps working when you are alone, a device that makes people predictable fails the moment others defect, and a device that makes your own future self predictable fails when you change.

  2. Where the structure physically resides

    In matter — marks, objects, built form; in behavioural regularity — what people reliably do; or in a symbolic system whose elements have conventional significance. This is not a pedantic distinction. It determines what it costs to maintain the scaffold and what destroys it: matter decays, regularities erode when unenforced, and symbolic systems fail through drift in what the symbols mean.

  3. How it does its work

    By constraining — reducing what can happen, so less needs predicting; by offloading — holding information so the model need not; or by retraining — reshaping the model itself, so that what a person can think changes. The third is the strongest and rarest, and it is where Clark's treatment of language lands.

Note

These axes are the site's, not the book's. Chapter 9 proceeds thematically — language, then sociality, then institutions — and does not distinguish constraining from offloading from retraining, although the distinction is doing implicit work throughout and the chapter's most interesting claims are all in the third category. The placements below are derived from the chapter's material; where a placement extends beyond what the chapter argues, it says so.

Marks and notations

The simplest case and the one Clark's earlier work made familiar. A note on a scrap of paper reduces uncertainty about the world and about your own future state — specifically about what you will know in three hours. It resides in matter, and it works by offloading: the mark holds what the model would otherwise have to. Its failure mode follows from its placement. Matter decays and gets lost, and because it is pure offloading rather than retraining, losing it leaves the model exactly as it was before, with nothing internalised.

Language

The most interesting placement, because language is the only scaffold in the landscape that sits substantially in the retraining column. Clark's claim is not merely that words let us tell each other things, though they do, and that is offloading of a powerful kind. It is that a learned label acts as a high-level prior — a compact hypothesis that, once available, reshapes what the generative model predicts. Acquiring a word for a category installs an expectation, and the expectation then structures perception in the ordinary top-down way. Words do not simply describe the carved-up world; they participate in carving it.

This is where the framework connects to a very old set of questions about linguistic relativity, and Clark's version is more defensible than the usual ones because it specifies a mechanism: a word is a prior with a precision, so its influence should be graded, should depend on context, and should be strongest exactly where the sensory evidence is weakest. That last prediction is a real one, and it fits the pattern of the categorical-perception literature better than strong or weak Whorfianism stated without machinery. The best-known demonstration is Jonathan Winawer and colleagues' 2007 study in PNAS: Russian obligatorily distinguishes siniy from goluboy, darker and lighter blue, where English has one word. Russian speakers discriminated two blues faster when the pair straddled that lexical boundary than when both fell inside one category; English speakers showed no such advantage on the identical stimuli. Crucially, the Russian advantage disappeared when participants performed a concurrent verbal task, and survived a comparable spatial task — which is what a lexical prior competing for the same resources should do.

Norms and conventions

Uncertainty about other people; resident in behavioural regularity; working by constraint. A convention does not tell you anything about the world — it narrows what people will do, so that less needs predicting. The placement explains the characteristic phenomenology: conventions are nearly invisible while they hold and produce sharp discomfort when broken, which is what you would expect of a device whose whole function is to keep prediction error at zero. It also explains their fragility. Because they reside in regularity rather than in matter or symbol, they exist only as long as enough people keep instantiating them, and they can collapse quickly once the expectation of compliance goes.

Institutions

Law, money, timetables, the working week. These reduce uncertainty about other people at a scale and over a timespan that norms cannot reach, and they are the only entries that occupy all three columns of axis 2 at once — behavioural regularity, backed by symbolic representation, anchored in matter. That redundancy is what buys their durability, and it is why an institution can survive the defection of many individuals where a convention cannot.

Their position on axis 3 shifts over time, which is the most interesting thing the grid shows about them. An institution begins by constraining behaviour and ends by offloading cognition: once the working week is established, nobody predicts when others will be available, they simply read it off the structure. This readthrough treats the shift as the site's extension of the chapter's argument rather than as a claim Clark makes.

The built environment

Roads, doors, kerbs, signage, the shapes of rooms. Uncertainty about the physical world, and about people by way of it; resident in matter; working by constraint. A corridor reduces the space of where a person may be next more effectively than any norm, and it does so without requiring anyone to have learned anything. This is the purest case of what the framework calls making the world predictable rather than modelling it better, and it is the point at which the account touches architecture and design — briefly, in the book, and suggestively rather than substantively.

What the axes reveal

The scaffolds are not interchangeable, and the chapter's enthusiasm can make them look it. Offloading and retraining differ in what happens when the scaffold is removed: lose your notes and you are where you started, but a language you learned has changed the model, and the change persists. Only language is substantially in the retraining column, which is a real asymmetry and an argument for treating it as more than one scaffold among many.

Constraint is doing more work than offloading. The extended mind literature, Clark's own included, has focused on the notebook — on external storage. The grid suggests that the larger contribution of human culture is not that it remembers for us but that it narrows the world, so that far less needs predicting in the first place. That is the laziness of Part 9 operating at civilisational scale.

Now the placement test, on three things the chapter never mentions. A recipe: uncertainty about the world and about your own future state, resident in symbol and matter, working by offloading — with a component of retraining for a cook who internalises it. Money: uncertainty about other people, resident across all three, working by constraint that has become so complete it now reads as offloading — nobody negotiates the value of a coin. A traffic light: uncertainty about other people, resident in matter and convention jointly, working by constraint — and its failure mode is precisely the one the axes predict for a scaffold split between matter and behavioural regularity, since it fails both when the bulb goes and when drivers stop complying.

Chapter 10: what Clark thinks is still wrong

The closing chapter is short and is the reason this readthrough marks it condensed: it advances no new argument, gathering instead what Clark calls the outstanding problems, puzzles and pitfalls. Its substance belongs partly here and partly in the afterword, where the site's own assessment lives and cannot contaminate the reading.

What is worth carrying from it is the list itself, because a framework's author naming its weak points is more informative than any summary. Clark flags the unresolved status of how literally the neural implementation should be taken — whether cortex really contains the segregated error and representation populations that Part 2 requires. He flags the difficulty of specifying the innate expectations that joint 2 of Part 9 depends on. He acknowledges that the framework's breadth invites the charge of unfalsifiability and does not claim to have disposed of it. And he is explicit that predictive processing as it stands is a research programme rather than a finished theory — that the unification is a promissory note whose value depends on work not yet done.

That candour is worth registering precisely because the preceding nine chapters do not sound like this. A reader who stops at chapter 9 gets a considerably more confident book than the one Clark actually wrote.

What "surfing" was meant to convey

The title has been waiting since the preface, and it repays being taken literally. A surfer is not fighting the wave and not computing it. They are not, at any point, in a stable state that they return to after disturbances — there is no equilibrium in surfing, only a continuous, active accommodation to a moving structure, in which staying upright and moving forward are the same activity. The alternative image, which the whole book is written against, is the brain as a receiver: sitting still, taking in what arrives, processing it, responding.

Every strand of the argument is in the metaphor. The surfer's competence is anticipatory — read the wave slightly ahead or fall. It is embodied, since the board and the water are participants rather than inputs. It is a matter of staying in the region you can predict rather than of responding well to surprises, which is the skill claim from Part 6. And the uncertainty in the title is not an obstacle to be eliminated but the medium being ridden: a wave is precisely a structured, moving, never-quite-repeating thing, and a creature adapted to it is one that never lands.

It is a good metaphor and it is also, in a way worth noticing, an argument in disguise. The image makes fluid, continuous, embodied engagement feel like the obvious reading of the formalism — when, as Part 7 showed, exactly the same equations support Hohwy's picture of a brain sealed behind an evidentiary boundary, inferring a world it never touches. Nobody would title that book Surfing Uncertainty. The metaphor is doing philosophical work, and it does it so agreeably that the work is easy to miss.

Voice

Chapter 9 is the most speculative writing in the book and reads like it — Clark is extending rather than arguing, and the register is closer to his essays than to the careful chapter 6. Chapter 10 changes key entirely: short, plain, and unusually free of the metaphors that carry the rest. The contrast between the two is the clearest evidence in the book that Clark knows exactly how much he has and has not established.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that cultural structures can be described in the framework's vocabulary without strain, and that describing them that way makes at least one non-obvious distinction visible — between scaffolds that hold information and scaffolds that change the model. Also, in chapter 10, that the book's author holds his own conclusions more loosely than his chapters do.

Asserted: essentially all of chapter 9's substantive content. That norms and institutions are error-minimising devices rather than merely describable as such is offered without evidence that would distinguish it from any other functional account of culture, and no prediction is made that a cultural evolutionist or an institutional economist would not already expect. This is the chapter where the framework's vocabulary is most clearly doing work as a lens rather than as a mechanism, and it is the weakest chapter in the book on the ratio of claims to support.

Notes

The retraining claim is the chapter's most interesting and it rests on plausibility where it could have rested on data — the colour-vocabulary literature is the obvious support and the chapter does not use it. That literature has also moved since 2016, and in a direction worth knowing. A 2020 study in Cognition by Jasna Martinovic, Galina Paramei and W. Joseph MacInnes revisited the Russian blues effect and reported, in its title, the limits of language influencing colour discrimination: the effect is real but narrower and more condition-dependent than the 2007 result was often taken to show. That is the correct shape for this kind of finding, and it is also what the predictive account predicts — a lexical prior should matter most where sensory evidence is weak and wash out where it is strong. A reader should treat the graded, contested version as the state of play rather than the clean headline.
