The imaginarium: the machine run with the world switched off

Where we are

The hierarchy is built (Part 2) and the weighting term that governs how much sensory evidence counts is installed (Part 3). This part does something economical with both: it takes the machine and turns sensory precision down towards zero, and finds imagination, dreaming and memory arriving without any new apparatus. Assumes Part 3 throughout — the whole chapter is one parameter being moved.

First, a percept caught in the act of being a hypothesis

Before running the machine offline it helps to see the online version doing something that only makes sense if the percept really is a hypothesis rather than a construction from data. Present a different image to each eye — vertical stripes to the left, horizontal to the right — and hold them there. What people report is not a blend, not a plaid, and not a stable superimposition. It is alternation: vertical for a few seconds, then horizontal, then vertical again, indefinitely, with brief mixed transitions. This is binocular rivalry, and it has been studied since the nineteenth century.

On a data-assembly account the alternation is odd. Both signals are present continuously and equally, and a system combining incoming features has no reason to periodically discard half of them. On the prediction account it is close to obligatory. The generative model works by positing a scene that would produce the sensory data, and there is no single scene in which one surface is both vertically and horizontally striped at the same location. So no single hypothesis can explain away both streams. The system settles on the hypothesis that explains one of them, at which point the unexplained stream keeps producing large residuals, pressure builds, and the winning hypothesis is displaced by its rival — which now leaves the first stream unexplained, and the cycle repeats. What you experience as alternation is a competition between hypotheses that cannot both win.

Hold on to the implication: the percept is the winning hypothesis. Not a thing built from data and then interpreted — the interpretation itself. Everything in the rest of this part follows from asking what happens to that hypothesis when the data stops arguing with it.

The ratchet

  1. A perception engine that cannot imagine

    What we have
    An inference machine driven by sensory data: predictions descend, residuals ascend, estimates settle. Every state it enters is a response to something arriving at a sensory surface.
    What breaks
    Human beings routinely occupy richly perceptual states with no corresponding input at all. Close your eyes and visualise your front door. You dream, in detail, for roughly an hour and a half a night, with the eyes shut and the sensory gates largely closed. People who lose their sight to eye disease frequently begin to see elaborate, formed, unwilled visual scenes — a condition described by Charles Bonnet in the eighteenth century and named after him — while remaining entirely lucid and knowing perfectly well the scenes are not there. A pure perception engine has nothing to say about any of this. The obvious repair, bolting on a separate imagery system, is very expensive: it would need its own model of how the world looks, which is to say a duplicate of the generative model the perception system already has.
    The fix
    Add nothing. The generative model's native competence is running forwards, from hypothesised causes to expected sensory consequences; ordinarily that output is consumed by being compared against real input. Withdraw the input — or equivalently, drop the precision assigned to it — and the same downward cascade has nothing to be checked against. It does not stop. It becomes the content of experience.
    What it costs
    Any principled boundary between perceiving and imagining. On this design they are the same process at different settings, differing in degree rather than in kind — which is a strong and testable claim, and an uncomfortable one.

  2. Free-running gives you dreaming, not imagining

    What we have
    Sensory precision at the floor, model running unopposed.
    What breaks
    What that produces is undirected. It is a model settling into whatever states it finds most probable, which is a decent description of dreaming and of the deprivation hallucinations in Part 1, and a poor description of imagination. Imagining is deliberate and specific: asked to picture a red bicycle you produce a red bicycle, not the statistically most likely scene.
    The fix
    Drive the hierarchy from the top instead of letting it settle from the bottom. A high-level state is imposed — by instruction, by intention, by the demands of a task — and the descending cascade elaborates it downwards into the detailed sensory pattern it would produce. Same pathway, same model, different origin for the state that starts it.
    What it costs
    Imagery and perception must now compete for the same machinery, so the design predicts they should interfere with each other. That is a risky prediction, and it is old news: in 1910, working in Titchener's laboratory at Cornell, Cheves West Perky asked participants to visualise an everyday object on a blank screen while, unknown to them, a very faint image of that object was actually projected onto it. They did not notice the real stimulus. They reported their own imagery — and described it in terms that tracked the projection they denied seeing.1 The prediction survives its oldest test.

  3. Memory has no records to retrieve

    What we have
    A model that can be run forwards, from above, without input.
    What breaks
    The standard picture of remembering is retrieval: an episode was encoded, stored, and is now read back, more or less degraded. But this machine has no store of episodes. It has a model — a set of weights encoding how the world tends to produce sensory consequences. There is nothing in it to retrieve.
    The fix
    Remembering is the same operation as imagining, constrained by a cue. To recall yesterday's meeting is to drive the generative model into the state that would produce the sensory consequences that meeting produced — to re-generate the episode rather than to read it. Memory becomes constructive by architecture rather than by unfortunate degradation.
    What it costs
    Accuracy, systematically and unavoidably. A reconstruction is filled in by the model's general expectations wherever the cue underdetermines the episode, which means memories will drift towards the typical and will absorb plausible material presented afterwards. That is not a regrettable imperfection on this account; it is the signature of the mechanism working. Frederic Bartlett's Remembering (1932) documented exactly this pattern of drift towards the culturally expected, and a large later literature on misinformation effects has done little but confirm it.

One dial, three states of mind

Run the machine three times on identical inputs, changing nothing but sensory precision. Prior precision is held at π_prior = 1.00 throughout; the prior expects 0.90; the sensory channel is delivering a weak 0.10. As in Part 3, the weight on a channel is its share of the total precision, and the result is the weighted average.

  1. Ordinary perception. Sensory precision π_s = 1.00, equal to the prior's. The sensory weight is 1.00 ÷ (1.00 + 1.00) = 0.50. The percept is 0.50 × 0.10 + 0.50 × 0.90 = 0.05 + 0.45 = 0.50 — a genuine compromise between what is expected and what is arriving, which is what waking perception is.
  2. Deliberate imagery. Attention is withdrawn from the channel and its precision falls to π_s = 0.25. The sensory weight drops to 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 0.20, and the percept moves to 0.20 × 0.10 + 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.02 + 0.72 = 0.74. Experience is now dominated by the model, but the world still has a vote — which is why imagery in a lit room is typically thin and unstable, and why people close their eyes to imagine.
  3. Dreaming, or hallucination. Sensory precision reaches π_s = 0.00. The sensory weight is exactly 0.00, and the percept is 0.90 — the prior, unmodified. The arriving 0.10 is still physically present and now contributes nothing whatsoever. Note that the machine did not disconnect the senses; it stopped counting them, which produces the same result by a different route.
  4. Read the whole run together. There is no step in this sequence where the machinery changes. The difference between seeing your kitchen, picturing your kitchen, and dreaming your kitchen is the value of a single scalar. That is the chapter's entire claim, and it is why the framework can absorb imagery, dreaming, Charles Bonnet hallucinations and the blindfold results of Part 1 without adding any structure.

The physiology of sleep obliges pleasingly. During REM sleep, sensory transmission to cortex is substantially gated and skeletal motor output is actively inhibited — the two conditions this account requires for the model to run unchecked and for the resulting predictions not to be acted upon.

Grip, and where meaning comes from

Chapter 3 closes with a more speculative move that is easy to miss and matters for Part 7. If the brain's model is a model of how causes generate sensory consequences, then what the model is about is causal structure in the world — not a picture of appearances but a grip on what produces what. Clark's suggestion is that this is where semantic content comes from: the states of the model mean what they do because of the causal structure they track and the actions that structure supports.

The qualification he attaches is the interesting part. The model does not track causal structure indiscriminately, because there is infinitely much of it. It tracks the structure that matters to an organism of this kind with these needs — the patterns that make a difference to what it should do. Meaning, on this account, is not correspondence to the world in general but purchase on the parts of it that bear on action. This is Clark reconnecting predictive processing to the embodied tradition, and it is the first appearance of a move the whole of Part II of the book depends on.

Note

The imagery-perception continuity claim has a strong version and a weak one, and the chapter does not always separate them. The weak version — that imagery and perception share representational resources and interfere with each other — is well supported, by the Perky effect and by a large modern literature using imagery to bias binocular rivalry. The strong version — that they are the same process differing only in a precision setting — is harder, because imagery and perception dissociate in ways a single dial does not obviously predict. People with aphantasia report no voluntary visual imagery at all while perceiving normally, and some patients show the reverse profile. A single parameter should not produce clean double dissociations, and the honest position is that the framework accommodates these by positing separate precision settings for separate pathways, which is exactly the sort of after-the-fact flexibility its critics complain about.

Voice

The chapter title is doing the work Clark's titles usually do: an imaginarium is a place where images are made, and the joke is that it turns out to be the same room as the one where perception happens. This is the most exuberant chapter in Part I, and the least constrained — the material moves quickly across dreaming, imagery, memory and meaning, and Clark is visibly enjoying how much falls out of one idea. It is also where a careful reader should slow down, since the density of things explained per page of evidence supplied is higher here than anywhere before it.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that a generative-model architecture has the resources to produce perceptual states without input, that this is an economy rather than an add-on, and that the resulting predictions about imagery-perception interference were confirmed a century before the framework existed.

Asserted: that this is how imagination, dreaming and memory actually work in human beings. The chapter demonstrates sufficiency — the machine could do these things this way — and then reads sufficiency as explanation. The reconstructive-memory material is the strongest, because Bartlett's pattern of drift is specifically predicted rather than merely accommodated. The dreaming material is the weakest: that dreams are the generative model running unchecked is consistent with almost everything known about dreaming and predicted by almost nothing.

Notes

Charles Bonnet syndrome deserves more than the framework's tidy explanation of it, because it is a case where the tidy explanation may be doing harm. It is common — estimates in populations with significant vision loss run to a substantial minority — and it is chronically under-reported, because patients fear that describing vivid hallucinations will be taken as evidence of dementia or psychosis. The predictive account is genuinely useful here, since it says plainly that these hallucinations are what a healthy visual system does when starved of input, and that lucidity is expected rather than surprising. That is a case of a theoretical framework earning its keep clinically, and it is worth recording alongside the criticisms.

What you carry forward

The dial, and the three positions on it. The idea that the percept is the winning hypothesis, established by rivalry before it was needed for imagery. And Clark's first statement of where content comes from — causal structure, filtered by what matters to an organism that has to act. That last point is not decoration. It is the hinge into Part II of the book, where Part 5 collapses the distinction between perceiving the world and doing things in it.
