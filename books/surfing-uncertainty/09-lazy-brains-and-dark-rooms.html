The lazy brain, and the dark room it never enters

Where we are

Opens Part III of the book. The machinery is complete and has been turned outward, inward and clinical. This chapter asks what kind of organism the framework describes, dissolves the familiar division of reasoning into two systems, reconnects the whole thing to Clark's own extended-mind thesis, and finally confronts the objection that has been outstanding since Part 5 made action a matter of minimising surprise.

Laziness as a design principle

The chapter's organising thought is that prediction error minimisation is not free, and a system that must pay for it will pay as little as it can. Detailed internal modelling is expensive. Sensorimotor shortcuts of the kind that catch a fly ball in Part 7 are cheap. Letting a structure in the environment hold information so you do not have to is cheaper still. A brain organised around minimising error subject to cost will therefore keep reaching for the cheapest resource that keeps error tolerable — and, crucially, it already has the machinery to decide which resource that is, because precision is exactly a device for shifting influence between channels.

This is why the chapter is called what it is, and the laziness is not a slur. It is the claim that a predictive brain is a strategic organ rather than a maximally powerful one, and that the interesting question about any cognitive achievement is not how the brain computed it but how much of it the brain avoided having to compute.

Why there are not two systems

The most familiar framework in modern psychology divides thinking in two: a fast, automatic, intuitive process and a slow, effortful, deliberative one, popularised as System 1 and System 2 by Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow in 2011 and mirrored in computational neuroscience by the distinction between model-free learning, which caches the value of actions, and model-based learning, which simulates consequences using a model of the world. The standard laboratory instrument for separating the two is the two-step task introduced by Nathaniel Daw, Samuel Gershman, Ben Seymour, Peter Dayan and Ray Dolan in Neuron in 2011, which measures how far a person's choices are sensitive to the structure of the task rather than to cached reward. Clark's argument is that these labels pick out real differences in behaviour and do not pick out two kinds of machinery.

On the predictive account, what look like two systems are two settings of the same one. A high-precision sensory channel and a low-precision model give you fast, stimulus-driven, apparently automatic behaviour. Shift the weighting towards the model and towards its slower, more abstract upper levels, and you get deliberation: internally generated trajectories evaluated before acting, which is the machinery of Part 4 put to work on the future. Clark's phrase for what the labels actually name is "different admixtures of resource and influence", each recruited in the same general way as circumstances dictate. The dichotomy is a description of the endpoints of a continuum, and it has been mistaken for an inventory of parts.

And why the mind leaks

The same laziness reconnects the framework to the thesis Clark is best known for. If a brain recruits whatever resource is cheapest, and if precision-weighting is the mechanism by which resources are recruited, then there is nothing in the machinery that stops the recruitment at the skull. A notebook that reliably holds an address, a tool whose dynamics have become predictable, a colleague who can be trusted to remember the schedule — each is a channel whose error signals can be assigned high precision and relied upon, and the cost-cutting logic that favours a sensorimotor shortcut over internal simulation favours these for the same reason.

Clark's claim is not that predictive processing entails the extended mind. It is the more careful one that the framework is, in his words, "a specific, and highly extension-friendly" proposal about what the brain contributes — that the neural contribution it describes is precisely the kind that slots into larger circuits rather than sealing itself off. Whether that is the right reading is the dispute with Jakob Hohwy set out in Part 7, and this chapter does not resolve it so much as show what the extension-friendly reading buys once you accept it.

The joint map: answering the dark room

Now the objection. If an organism acts to minimise the surprise associated with its sensory states, the optimal strategy appears obvious and appalling: find a small dark quiet room, stay in it, and never be surprised again. Clark names this the Darkened Room Puzzle and states it in its strongest form — that prediction-driven creatures should seek out easily predicted states and spend the remainder of their increasingly hungry and thirsty days there. It is not a debating point. It is the most-raised objection to the free-energy framework, and if it succeeds the whole apparatus describes creatures that could not survive.

The answer has four conditions.

  1. What is minimised is expected surprise over time

    Not the surprise of the current instant. The quantity is an expectation taken across a trajectory into the future, and a trajectory that begins in a dark room ends in starvation. Starvation is, for a creature with a model of itself, an extremely surprising state — a violent departure from the sensory conditions it expects to occupy. The dark room minimises surprise now by guaranteeing enormous surprise later, and the quantity being minimised already includes the later.

  2. The model comes with expectations it did not learn

    The load-bearing condition. Joint 1 only works if the organism expects to be fed, warm, upright, and intact — otherwise starvation is not a departure from anything. Clark's answer is that these expectations are structurally implicit and innately specified: built into the organism's model by evolution rather than acquired, encoding the states a creature of this kind occupies when it is doing well. On this account a species' phenotype is a set of expectations about its own sensory trajectory, and staying alive is keeping those expectations fulfilled.

  3. Those expectations are high-precision and not revisable away

    Without this the answer collapses, because a system that can revise any prediction could simply revise the expectation of being fed and then be perfectly content to starve. These deep expectations sit at the top of the hierarchy — where, as Part 2 noted, there is nothing above to correct them — and carry precision high enough that ordinary evidence cannot move them.

  4. Reducing uncertainty is itself error-minimising

    The final piece, and the one that turns a defensive answer into a positive account. Epistemic action from Part 6 — moving in order to resolve uncertainty rather than to change the world — reduces expected error going forward. A creature that explores its environment while conditions are good is buying a better model, and a better model means less surprise later. Play, curiosity and novelty-seeking are therefore not exceptions to the imperative that need excusing. They are instances of it.

The joints are conjunctive, and joint 2 is carrying the argument. Joints 1, 3 and 4 are all machinery for keeping certain expectations satisfied, and none of them specifies which expectations. Remove joint 2 and the objection returns immediately and undefeated: a creature with no built-in expectations about its own trajectory has nothing to be surprised by in a dark room, and the dark room is optimal after all. Everything hangs on there being a set of unlearned, unrevisable expectations about what it is to be an organism of this kind — which means the answer to the dark room is, at bottom, a claim about innate content.

A creature that does not hide

Run a foraging animal — call it a rat in an unfamiliar enclosure — through the joints in order.

  1. Joint 2 supplies the standing expectations. Before any learning about this enclosure, the animal's model already encodes the sensory signature of a rat that is doing well: a certain range of blood glucose, a body temperature near 37 °C, gut distension within limits, a hydration state, an intact skin. These are not beliefs it acquired. They are what being this kind of animal consists in, written as expectations.
  2. Joint 1 evaluates the dark corner. The enclosure has a covered corner where sensory input is minimal and nothing changes. Instantaneous surprise there is very low. But the quantity being minimised runs forward, and the forward trajectory from that corner passes through rising interoceptive error as glucose falls — a state the model of joint 2 assigns very low probability. The corner scores badly precisely because the evaluation is not myopic.
  3. Joint 3 stops the cheap escape. The system could in principle resolve the mismatch the way Part 5 warned about — by revising the prediction, concluding that low blood glucose is fine, and remaining in the corner. It cannot, because those expectations sit at the top of the hierarchy with precision high enough to be immune to the evidence. The prediction wins and the animal must act.
  4. Joint 4 explains what it does while it is not hungry. Well-fed and safe, the animal still does not settle in the corner: it explores, rears, sniffs, and returns to places it has already been. On the framework's account each of those movements is an epistemic action that reduces uncertainty about the enclosure — building the model that will make future foraging cheaper. The behaviour that looks most like a counterexample to surprise-minimisation is the clearest instance of it, once the expectation is taken over a trajectory.
  5. The outcome. An animal that eats, explores, and returns to shelter without ever occupying the state that the naive reading of the framework predicted. Note what did the work: not a drive, not a reward signal, not a utility function, but the content of a set of expectations the animal never chose.

When the room does get dark

The contrast case is uncomfortable and it is the reason to take joint 2 seriously. Consider a condition in which joints 1, 3 and 4 are intact but the expectations of joint 2 are weakened — where the model no longer strongly anticipates the sensory signature of a creature doing well, or assigns those anticipations reduced precision.

What does the framework then predict? Exactly what the objection described. Reduced exploration, because epistemic action only pays if a better model serves expectations you actually hold. Withdrawal to low-stimulation environments, because these now genuinely do minimise expected surprise. Reduced responsiveness to food and social contact, because the states they restore are no longer strongly expected. A flattening of the difference between doing well and doing badly.

That is a recognisable description of severe depression, and the computational literature has developed accounts along broadly these lines — withdrawal and anhedonia as what an inference system does when its expectations about its own future states are attenuated. The record agrees with the prediction, and the agreement is what establishes joint 2 as load-bearing: the dark room is not impossible for this framework. It is what the framework predicts when one specific condition fails, and human beings do sometimes enter it.

Two things follow. The dark-room objection is answered rather than dissolved — Clark's reply works, but it works by locating the answer in innate content rather than in the dynamics, which is a weaker and more contingent result than the confident tone of the chapter suggests. And the framework acquires a genuine explanatory success it did not go looking for, since the objection's own scenario turns out to name a real clinical state.

Note

The dark-room answer has been criticised as circular, and the criticism deserves its strongest form. The framework's claim is that all behaviour flows from one imperative: minimise expected prediction error. But joint 2 supplies, as innate expectations, precisely the content that a more traditional theory would supply as drives and goals. Critics ask what has been gained by relabelling hunger as an expectation of satiety — and note that the framework gives no independent way to determine which expectations an organism innately has, so the expectations can always be read off the behaviour they are invoked to explain. Clark's defence is that the unification is real because the same machinery now handles perception, action and motivation, and because the free-energy formulation gives the expectations a principled home rather than an ad hoc one. The exchange has not been settled, and a reader should notice that it is the same elasticity objection from Part 3 arriving in a new domain.

How the cases were selected. The dual-process material is argued rather than evidenced — Clark is making a conceptual case that a distinction has been reified, and he does not marshal experiments that discriminate one-system from two-system accounts, largely because the field has not produced decisive ones. For the dark room, the cases are chosen from foraging and exploratory behaviour, where the answer works cleanly. Behaviours that are harder for the account — self-destructive habits, addiction, deliberate risk-seeking — are not put through the same test in this chapter.

What the claim buys and pays. It buys the unification that the whole book has been building towards: one imperative covering perception, action, deliberation, motivation and the recruitment of external resources. It pays in a specific and now-familiar currency. The answer to the dark room works by putting the explanatory weight into innate expectations whose content is not independently specifiable — so the framework can explain why creatures forage, and equally why some withdraw, without any prior commitment as to which it should predict. That is the elasticity trade made once more, at the highest level yet.

Voice

Chapter 8 is the most argumentative chapter in the book — Clark is no longer showing the framework absorbing phenomena but defending it against a named objection, and the prose tightens accordingly. It is also where he is most visibly a partisan of his own earlier work, and the extended-mind section reads as a homecoming. The Darkened Room discussion is placed at the very end of the chapter, which has the effect of treating the field's most persistent objection as a coda rather than a crisis.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established: that the two-systems vocabulary is not entailed by the evidence it is usually taken to summarise, and that a single-mechanism account with variable precision can produce the behavioural signatures that motivated the dichotomy. Also that exploration is not prima facie inconsistent with surprise-minimisation, which is a real result — the objection in its crude form does fail.

Asserted: the existence and content of the innate expectations that joint 2 requires. Nothing in the chapter measures them, derives them, or says how one would find out whether a given organism has a particular one. Clark writes as though phenotype-as-expectation were an established reformulation; it is a proposal, and it is the proposal on which his answer to the book's most serious objection entirely depends.

Notes

The dual-process material is worth a note because the ground has shifted since 2016. Clark's argument that model-free and model-based learning are not distinct systems has been substantially supported by subsequent work using that same two-step task, which found not two populations of subject but a continuum — every participant showing a mixture of both influences, with the balance moving under task demands, stress and cognitive load. That is a case where the book backed the right horse. The broader System 1 / System 2 framing has also weakened for reasons unconnected to predictive processing — several of the specific findings that popularised it have failed to replicate — so a reader should not treat Clark's target as the consensus it was when he was writing against it.

What you carry forward

Laziness as the organising principle of Part III: a brain that recruits the cheapest adequate resource, with precision as the recruiting mechanism. The dissolution of the two-systems picture. The dark-room answer and, more importantly, the location of its weak point at joint 2. Part 10 takes the recruitment outward one final step — to language, norms and institutions as things we build to make each other predictable — and closes with what Clark himself thinks is still wrong with the picture.
