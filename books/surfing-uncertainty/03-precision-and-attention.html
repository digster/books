Precision, and why attention turns out to be the same knob

Where we are

Part 2 built a hierarchy in which descending predictions are subtracted from ascending signals and only the residual travels forward, and left one debt unpaid twice over: a residual is worthless until the system decides how far to trust it. This part pays that debt. It is the most important page in this readthrough, because Parts 4, 5, 8 and 9 are all applications of the single parameter introduced here, and none of them re-derives it.

The ratchet

  1. Error at face value, and the fog that destroys it

    What we have
    The machine as Part 2 left it. A prediction descends, the sensory signal arrives, the difference ascends, and the estimate above is revised in proportion to that difference. Nothing about this is obviously incomplete — it is exactly what a least-squares fitting procedure does, and it works.
    What breaks
    Put the system in fog. The visual signal is now mostly noise: a weak shape somewhere inside a great deal of random variation, and the residual it generates is large. Taken at face value, that large residual is a strong instruction to revise the model of the world — and the model duly revises, on the strength of noise. Run it forward and the consequence is not inefficiency, it is catastrophe: the system hallucinates shapes into the mist and then, because the revised model now predicts those shapes, finds its own inventions confirmed. The same failure has a quieter everyday form. In dim light the retina's own photon noise produces exactly the same kind of large, meaningless residual, and a system that trusted it would be least reliable precisely when it most needs to be careful.
    The fix
    Do not act on error, act on error scaled by how much the channel it came from is currently worth listening to. That scaling term is precision, and it is defined as the inverse of the expected variance of the channel: a signal with small expected noise has high precision and is taken seriously; a signal with large expected noise has low precision and is nearly ignored. The update becomes precision-weighted, and the foggy channel stops being able to bully the model.
    What it costs
    A second inference problem. The system does not come equipped with the variances of its own channels; it has to estimate them, from the same stream it is already trying to explain.

  2. A fixed weight per sense, and why it is wrong every time

    What we have
    The obvious implementation: give each modality a reliability coefficient. Vision is generally sharper than touch for spatial judgements, so weight vision more. Set the constants once, perhaps over evolutionary time, and be done.
    What breaks
    Reliability is not a property of a channel; it is a property of a channel at this moment, in this context. Vision is superb at noon and nearly useless in fog, and the ratio between vision and touch inverts completely between those two conditions. Proprioception is dependable until the limb is numbed or the tendon is vibrated. A fixed coefficient is not approximately right — it is right for one lighting condition and wrong for all the others, and the cases where it is most wrong are exactly the dangerous ones.
    The fix
    Predict precision too. The expected reliability of each error channel becomes another quantity the generative model estimates, context by context, learned from experience and organised hierarchically like everything else — so that a higher level can predict, from the fact that it is foggy, that visual error signals should be discounted for the next while. Precision-weighting is not a fixed gain but a controlled one.
    What it costs
    Name it precisely, because it is the most consequential cost in the book: the model now decides how much to trust the evidence against it. That is not a rhetorical flourish — it is the literal structure of the scheme. It is what makes the machine robust, and it is what makes it possible for the machine to become unfalsifiable to itself.

  3. The knob turns out to be something we already had a word for

    What we have
    A controllable gain on every error channel, set by the system's own contextual estimate of reliability.
    What breaks
    Nothing breaks. Something is noticed, and this is the turn the whole book pivots on. Consider what a controllable gain on sensory channels actually does. Turn it up on one channel and that channel dominates the update, driving perception and learning; turn it down and it is functionally invisible, present in the signal but unable to influence anything. Selecting some sensory streams for influence while suppressing others, flexibly, according to task and context, is not a novel capability that needs explaining. It is a description of attention.
    The fix
    Do not build an attention module and bolt it on. Clark's claim is stronger and stranger than that: precision-weighting and attention are not two things, one of which explains the other — they are one operation described in two vocabularies, one computational and one psychological. Attending to something just is raising the precision on the error signals carrying information about it. The classic findings the attention literature accumulated — that attention enhances the gain of neural responses, that attended and unattended stimuli compete for representation in a way that attention biases — stop being facts about a separate spotlight and become facts about how this parameter is set.
    What it costs
    Independence, in both directions. Attention can no longer be studied as a faculty with its own machinery, and the identification now owes debts to a large empirical literature that was not built with it in mind. If some well-established attentional effect turns out not to be expressible as a gain change, the identification fails.

  4. A knob can be mis-set

    What we have
    One parameter, continuously variable, controlling the balance between what you expect and what you are receiving.
    What breaks
    Consider the extremes, which the machine permits and nothing prevents. Push sensory precision persistently down, or prior precision persistently up, and experience is increasingly determined by expectation rather than by input — which is a functional description of hallucination. Push it the other way, so that error signals are always taken at full strength, and nothing is ever explained away: every ordinary variation demands a model revision, the world stops being predictable, and it becomes, in a phrase that has stuck to this literature, too real.
    The fix
    There is no fix, because this is not a bug to be engineered out — it is a prediction. If attention and psychosis and autism are settings of one parameter, then the framework says specific things about all three, and those things can be tested. Part 8 is that territory.
    What it costs
    Falsifiability, arguably. One parameter that explains attention, dreaming, autism and schizophrenia is either a remarkable unification or a term flexible enough to accommodate any result. Both readings are live, and the afterword gives each its best form.

A ridge, seen and felt

One complete run, with real values. The setting is the experiment that made this arithmetic respectable: in 2002 Marc Ernst and Martin Banks reported in Nature that people combine visual and haptic estimates of an object's size in the way a statistician would — weighting each by its reliability — and that degrading vision with added noise shifts the weighting towards touch by exactly the predicted amount.

Take a raised ridge whose height a person both looks at and grips. Vision reports 57.0 mm; touch reports 53.0 mm. They disagree, as real senses do. The question the machine has to answer is not which one is right but how much each should count.

  1. Convert each channel's noise into precision. Precision is the reciprocal of the variance, and variance is the square of the standard deviation. In good light the visual estimate has σ = 1.5 mm, so its variance is 2.25 and its precision is 1 ÷ 2.25 = 0.4444. Touch has σ = 3.0 mm, variance 9.0, precision 1 ÷ 9.0 = 0.1111.
  2. Turn precisions into weights. Each channel's weight is its share of the total precision. Total precision is 0.4444 + 0.1111 = 0.5556. So vision gets 0.4444 ÷ 0.5556 = 0.80 and touch gets 0.1111 ÷ 0.5556 = 0.20. Vision is twice as precise in linear terms and therefore four times as influential, because precision goes as the square of the reliability. That squaring is the reason small differences in noise produce large differences in influence.
  3. Combine. The integrated estimate is the weighted average: 0.80 × 57.0 + 0.20 × 53.0 = 45.6 + 10.6 = 56.2 mm. The percept sits near vision but is pulled a little towards touch.
  4. Note what happened to the uncertainty. Precisions add: the combined precision is 0.5556, so the combined variance is 1 ÷ 0.5556 = 1.80 and the combined standard deviation is √1.80 = 1.34 mm. That is lower than either channel alone — better than vision's 1.5 mm. Two disagreeing senses produce an estimate more reliable than either. This is the pay-off that makes the whole scheme worth its cost, and it is the part most often left out of informal accounts.
  5. Now degrade the vision. Add visual noise so that σ = 6.0 mm. Visual variance becomes 36.0 and visual precision 1 ÷ 36.0 = 0.0278. Touch is unchanged at 0.1111. Total precision is now 0.1389.
  6. The weights invert, exactly. Vision gets 0.0278 ÷ 0.1389 = 0.20; touch gets 0.1111 ÷ 0.1389 = 0.80. The integrated estimate is 0.20 × 57.0 + 0.80 × 53.0 = 11.4 + 42.4 = 53.8 mm, and the combined standard deviation rises to √7.2 = 2.68 mm — the system is correctly less certain than before.
  7. Read what this means. Neither sense changed its report. The percept moved 2.4 mm and the dominant modality changed hands, purely because one number — the system's estimate of its own visual noise — was altered. That number is precision, and turning it is the operation that the rest of this book keeps performing.

The arithmetic just run is the whole content of the book's first appendix, Bare Bayes, in the only form most readers need: two estimates, each with an uncertainty, combined by adding precisions and averaging by precision share. That is Bayes' rule for Gaussian quantities. Everything else in the appendix is the general formulation of the same move.

Hearing Bing

The trace shows precision working. Clark's memorable case shows it failing, and it is the reason chapter 2 stays with a reader. In 2001 Harald Merckelbach and Vincent van de Ven played 44 undergraduate students a recording of white noise. They told them the recording might contain a faint snatch of Bing Crosby's White Christmas, and asked them to press a button when they heard it. There was no music on the tape at any point. 32% of them pressed the button at least once.1

Run it through the machine. A strong prior has been installed by the instruction — a specific expected auditory pattern, given high precision because the experimenter's assurance is credible. The incoming channel is white noise, which by construction has very low precision: nothing in it is reliable. High-precision prior, very low-precision evidence, and the update resolves overwhelmingly towards the prior. What the system delivers is not a judgement that the song is probably there. It is the experience of hearing it, because on this account the percept just is the winning hypothesis. Those who pressed the button scored higher on measures of fantasy proneness, which the framework reads as a standing disposition to weight priors heavily.

Put the blindfold study from Part 1 beside it and the pair covers both routes to the same outcome: drive sensory precision to the floor by removing the signal, or raise prior precision by supplying an expectation, and in both cases the model speaks unopposed. The mechanism does not distinguish hallucination from perception by any internal mark. It distinguishes them only by whether the winning hypothesis happened to be true.

Note

The identification of attention with precision is the book's boldest single move and it is not universally accepted, so it is worth being clear what would count against it. The proposal covers gain-like attentional phenomena very naturally — enhanced neural responses to attended stimuli, competition between stimuli biased by task set. It has a harder time with attentional effects that look structural rather than gain-like, such as feature binding and the serial character of visual search, where the question is not how strongly a signal counts but which things get bound to which. Clark treats these as absorbable; several attention researchers do not. Note also that Clark is not the originator here — the formal proposal that attention is precision optimisation was made by Harriet Feldman and Karl Friston in 2010, and Clark's contribution is to make it carry philosophical weight.

Voice

Chapter 2 is where Clark's prose comes alive, and the chapter title tells you how he is thinking about it: adjusting the volume, a physical knob on a physical device. He reaches for the everyday registration of the idea — the cocktail party, the noisy line, the expected knock — before the formal one, and the section headings are playful in a way the rest of the book is not. It is also where he first uses a move that recurs constantly afterwards: introduce a phenomenon everyone already has a name for, then show it falling out of the machinery, and let the reader supply the sense of inevitability themselves.

What this establishes, and what it asserts

Established, and quantitatively: that human sensory integration follows precision-weighted combination closely enough to be predicted numerically, and that manipulating a channel's noise shifts its influence by the predicted amount. Of everything in this book, the cue-combination evidence is the strongest — it is precise, replicated, and it makes a risky quantitative prediction rather than accommodating a qualitative pattern.

Asserted, and doing far more work than the evidence licenses: that the same quantity that governs cue combination in psychophysics is the quantity implemented by neuromodulatory gain control in cortex, and that it is identical to attention. Each step is plausible; the chain is long, and the later chapters lean on the chain rather than on its individual links. Clark states the identification with a confidence the empirical case does not supply. Where he says the framework explains attention, what has actually been shown is that a well-evidenced psychophysical principle and a well-evidenced neural phenomenon can be described in the same vocabulary.

Notes

On the White Christmas study: it has held up as a demonstration and has been repeated in several variants, but the specific figure should be handled with care. Rates of reported hearing vary substantially across replications depending on how the instruction is phrased and how the report is elicited — which is itself a point in the framework's favour, since the instruction is the prior being installed, but it means the number is a property of a procedure rather than a constant of human nature. The associated finding, that fantasy proneness predicts susceptibility, comes from a correlational design on a student sample and should not be read as establishing a mechanism.

A note on vocabulary that will save confusion later. In this literature "precision" is always the inverse of variance, but authors differ on what the precision is attached to. Precision on prediction error, precision on the prior, and precision on the likelihood are three different quantities that behave differently, and papers frequently move between them without warning. Clark is generally talking about precision on prediction error. When Part 8 reports competing accounts of autism that sound contradictory, the contradiction is often this ambiguity rather than a real disagreement.

What you carry forward

One knob, and the vocabulary for it: precision is inverse variance, precisions add, weights are precision shares, and the percept is the precision-weighted resolution of prior against evidence. The cue-combination trace, which is the thing to re-run mentally whenever a later claim about precision feels hand-wavy. And the identification — attention is this parameter, not a consequence of it. Part 4 takes the machine and drives sensory precision to zero, which turns out to give you imagination, dreaming and memory for free.
