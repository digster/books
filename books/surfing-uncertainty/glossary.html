Glossary

How to use this

This framework's vocabulary is unusually small — perhaps a dozen terms carry the whole apparatus — and unusually treacherous, because several of those terms are used to mean different things by different authors and occasionally by the same author in different chapters. The second section below is the more useful one: it lists the terms where the ambiguity is real, and says what the alternatives are. That is not pedantry. As the afterword records, the claim that this ambiguity is doing illegitimate unifying work is the most serious critique the framework currently faces.

The vocabulary

Active inference
Cancelling prediction error by acting on the world rather than by revising the model. Motor commands are recast as proprioceptive predictions, which reflex arcs then make true. See Part 5.
Action-oriented representation
Clark's term for the kind of content the framework posits: probabilistic, extended over time, and selected for what an organism must control rather than for accurate depiction of the world. His answer to the charge that predictive processing revives the internal replica. See Part 7.
Cognitive sandwich
The architecture the book argues against: perception in, cognition in the middle, action out. Active inference removes the middle, since perception and action become two directions of one operation.
Darkened Room Puzzle
The standing objection that a creature minimising surprise should seek a dark, quiet, unchanging room and stay there. Clark's answer runs through expected surprise over a trajectory plus innate expectations about one's own states. See Part 9.
Endstopping
A cell responding well to a short bar and less to a longer one — one of several extra-classical receptive-field effects (responses modulated by stimuli outside the classical receptive field). Awkward for feature detection; obligatory for an error unit. See Part 2.
Epistemic action
A movement performed to resolve uncertainty rather than to change the world — a saccade, a probing touch. Reduces expected future error, which is why exploration is an instance of the imperative rather than an exception to it.
Evidentiary boundary
Jakob Hohwy's term, not Clark's, for the division between a brain and everything it can only infer. Hohwy takes it to show the mind is skull-bound; Clark takes it to be real but mobile. The fault line of Part 7.
Explaining away
What a successful descending prediction does to an ascending signal: cancels it, so that nothing travels forward. A perfectly predicted stimulus is strongly present and forward-silent.
Free energy
A computable quantity that is an upper bound on surprisal. Minimising it pushes surprisal down without ever evaluating it. Under Gaussian assumptions it reduces to a sum of precision-weighted squared prediction errors — which is to say, to the machinery of Parts 2 and 3.
Generative model
An internal model that runs forwards, from hypothesised causes to their expected sensory consequences. Not a catalogue of appearances. Perception is the search for the hypothesis whose predicted consequences best cancel what arrived.
Hyperprior
An expectation at or near the top of the hierarchy, with nothing above it to correct it. Structurally insulated from revision by data, which is both what makes deep expectations stable and what makes a wrong one dangerous.
Hypo-priors
Pellicano and Burr's 2012 proposal that autistic perception involves attenuated priors, so experience is less shaped by expectation — the world becomes, in their phrase, too real. Contested; see Part 8.
Interoception
The sensory stream reporting the body's internal state — heart rate, gut, respiration, temperature. Treated by the framework exactly like any other channel, which is what lets it reach emotion and psychiatry. Distinguished from exteroception (the world) and proprioception (body position and movement).
Prediction error minimisation (PEM)
The single imperative the framework attributes to the brain. Often used interchangeably with "predictive processing," though strictly PEM names the objective and PP names the architecture that pursues it.
Precision
The inverse of the expected variance of a signal — a measure of how much a channel is currently worth listening to. Precisions add; weights are precision shares. The framework's most important term and its most overloaded one; see below and Part 3.
Sensory attenuation
The down-weighting of prediction error arising from one's own movements. Necessary for action to happen at all, responsible for the fact that you cannot tickle yourself, and — when it fails — for delusions of control.
Surprisal
The improbability of a sensory state given the model; formally the negative logarithm of its probability. Not a feeling. What an organism would ideally minimise, and what it cannot compute directly, which is why free energy exists.

Terms used inconsistently — read with care

These are the places where two papers can appear to disagree while saying the same thing, or appear to agree while saying different things.

Precision — on what?
The single most common source of confusion. Precision on prediction error, precision on the prior, and precision on the likelihood are three different quantities that behave differently. Clark generally means precision on prediction error. Because only the ratio between prior and sensory weighting determines an outcome, "weak priors" and "over-precise sensory channels" describe the same state — which is why several apparently rival accounts of autism are notational variants. See Part 8.
Attention
In this framework attention is not caused by precision-weighting or explained by it — it is identified with it. Authors who write that precision "modulates" attention are using a weaker claim than Clark's, sometimes without noticing. The identification is contested for structural attentional phenomena such as feature binding.
Hierarchy
Characterised variously as increasing spatiotemporal scope, increasing abstraction or gist, and increasing hyperprior status. Andrew Buskell's review argues these may be three distinct models sharing a name rather than three aspects of one. When a paper says "higher in the hierarchy," check which sense is meant.
Prediction
Always sub-personal and automatic, never a conscious expectation or judgement — Clark is explicit about this in chapter 1, and the everyday sense of the word imports a homunculus the framework does not intend. Note too that "prediction" here is about current hidden causes as often as about the future.
Forward model vs generative model
Not synonyms, though frequently treated as such. A forward model in classical motor control predicts sensory consequences from an efference copy of a genuine motor command. Active inference denies there are motor commands. Clark treats forward models as a special case absorbed by the general framework; many motor-control researchers do not. See Part 5.
Free energy principle vs predictive processing
The first is a general formal principle about systems that persist; the second is a specific architectural proposal about cortex. PP can be true if the FEP is empty, and the FEP can be true while cortex works some other way. Criticism aimed at one is routinely taken as criticism of the other.
