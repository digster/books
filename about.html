What these pages are

For each book, a set of pages that transmit what the book actually contains — its arguments, its machinery, its evidence, its cases, its arc — at enough depth and completeness that reading the pages gives you what reading the book would have given you.

What this is not

Not a review. There is no verdict here on whether a book is worth your time, and no thesis of our own competing with the book's. Evaluation does happen — but as a labelled layer sitting on top of the transmission, never mixed into it.

Not a summary. A summary tells you what a book is about. These pages tell you what is in it.

Not "key takeaways." Takeaways are the residue left behind after understanding. Distributing the residue and calling it the thing is the central failure this site exists to avoid.

The five tests

Every page is written against these, and the last three are the ones that separate having read about a thing from understanding it. No amount of fluent prose gets a reader past them.

Test Passes when Applies to
Caught-out You could talk about the book for an hour with someone who read it cover to cover and never be caught out — recall the case they raise, know the number they cite, notice when they misremember. every part
Particulars After any page you can name five specific things from it: a person, a place, a number, a study, an anecdote. every part
Whiteboard You could draw the mechanism from memory and narrate one complete operation through it, without notes. mechanism
Variation Someone changes a parameter — bigger pages, another level of hierarchy, a noisier signal — and you can say what happens and why. mechanism
Contrast Someone removes one of the claim's conditions and you can say what the claim then predicts, and whether the record agrees. claim
Placement Someone names an item the book never covers and you can locate it on the axes that organise the space. terrain

The particulars test is the cheapest and catches the most. Pages made entirely of concepts have failed, because concepts are what survives compression and particulars are what survives reading.

Three kinds of material

Each part declares its kind, not each book — genre is the wrong unit. A book can argue a claim in one chapter, explain a mechanism in the next, and lay out a landscape in the third, and each needs different apparatus to transmit. All three get equal treatment: a decomposition, a concrete run, an accounting, and a test that perturbs it.

Every chapter is accounted for

Each book's index carries a coverage table listing every chapter with one of four dispositions: full, condensed, folded, or read it — and anything short of the first states its reason.

Silently dropping a chapter is the one unrecoverable failure, because it converts I read the book into I read most of it and don't know which part I'm missing.

The honest limit

Some books, and some chapters, cannot be transmitted this way. Where the prose is the payload — where the effect comes from accumulation, from voice, from living inside an argument for three hundred pages — the page says plainly that this one has to be read, and says what specifically does not survive the transfer.

That admission is not a failure of the page. Concealing it would be.

Two more limits worth stating outright. These pages are written from a model of the book rather than alongside its text, which is what makes them a retelling rather than a paraphrase — but it also means they reorganise, and a reader wanting the author's own sequence will not find it here. And the evaluation layer is a judgment: it is labelled as one everywhere it appears, and you should read it as an argument rather than a finding.

How these pages are built

Hand-authored static HTML. No build step, no network requests — every page renders from the filesystem with the network off, and will still render in ten years. There is one small script, and all it does is remember whether you prefer the light or the dark theme. Turn JavaScript off and nothing is lost: the switch disappears and the pages follow your system's setting instead.

A block that sits above the page is the book's machinery being worked — a case run through an argument's joints, or a mechanism run on a concrete input. These are where understanding actually transfers, and they are what a reader scanning back through a part is almost always hunting for, so they are built to be found.

This is what one looks like

Lifted off the page, with a rule down its edge. The two kinds of worked run — the case walked and the trace — share one visual family because they do the same job in different material.

Three typefaces carry the same distinction a second time, so it survives greyscale, print, and a reader who never consciously notices grounds at all: a serif for the book (Literata, drawn for sustained screen reading), a sans for the site (Public Sans), and a monospace for the machinery — values, addresses, step numbers, code (JetBrains Mono). All three are self-hosted; nothing loads from a network.

The palette is a cool near-white paper with deep blue-black ink and one structural indigo, chosen partly for what it avoids. In dark mode every ground is redefined but their order is preserved — recessed stays darker than the page, raised stays lighter — which is why the rule above needs no second version for dark mode. The pages follow your system's setting until you use the switch in the header; after that they keep your choice, and flipping the switch back to match your system hands control back to it. On paper, where browsers omit background graphics, the same distinction is re-encoded as borders, and a page always prints light.
